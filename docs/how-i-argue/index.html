About me

Who I am and where I come from

I'm Nick. This page is about my family, what I believe, and the rules I try to hold myself to, so you can tell where the facts end and my opinion begins.

Who I am

I'm Nick. I'm Vietnamese American, I live in San Diego, and I'm a graduate student training to become a marriage and family therapist.

This blog is where I think out loud about the questions I can't stop asking. I'm a student sharing what I'm learning, so none of it is therapy or clinical advice.

Where I come from

My parents are both immigrants from Vietnam. My grandfather did construction work, and my mom worked hard enough to become a pharmacist. I grew up with my parents and siblings under one roof, in a family that valued school.

In my house, love looked like food on the table and long work hours. Hard feelings stayed quiet and private. I don't blame my parents, and context helps me understand their habits, but it doesn't erase how the silence felt to me.

My Vietnamese American background grounds me the most and gives me the most direction. Being Christian is part of who I am too, and I'm still grappling with what that means.

Counseling and Social Change

I studied psychology at San Diego State University and minored in Counseling and Social Change there. The minor put my own family next to everyone else's and gave me words for things I had only felt.

In my capstone class, we each wrote an “I Am” poem and read it out loud to the room. Writing mine was easy. Reading it in front of everyone was the hard part, and I'm still learning how much of who I am I leave unsaid.

The minor also got me looking at the systems around a person, along with the person. I'm still early in understanding that, and I get it wrong sometimes.

My mentors in philosophy taught me to ask the bigger questions behind everyday ones, like who decides what counts as normal. Most of what you read here, I learned from my professors at SDSU. These are not my original ideas.

Why I write the way I do

Some of what I write comes from frustration. I see families carrying pain they were taught not to talk about, and systems that miss people who need help. I've also been on the side that missed things.

When I dig into why something is broken, I usually find people doing their best under pressure, with only the view from their own seat. I have a seat too, and it has blind spots.

I also keep finding good. Parents work long hours so their kids have more choices. Teachers, neighbors and volunteers show up before any program does. So I try to name what's wrong, why it might happen, and one thing that could help, knowing I could be wrong.

I want to keep learning from my family, my classmates, my teachers and the people I'll work with, and keep asking before I assume.

Where I start from

Five ideals sit under every question here. They come from a simple picture of a good community: when hard times hit, neighbors help each other through.

Neighbors look after neighbors.

Storms, layoffs, hunger and family crises hit whole towns at once. I start from the belief that we owe each other help when they do.

Help now, and a way forward.

Good help meets the urgent need today and leaves people more able to run their own lives tomorrow. I try to judge policies by both.

Watch for who falls through the cracks.

Every rule and program misses someone. I try to name who, and why.

Everyone has something to give.

Nobody is only a receiver. A good system leaves room for people to give back, in time, skill or care.

Start local.

Big questions land on real streets. Neighbors, local businesses, libraries, faith groups and volunteers are often the first to show up, and I want to learn from them.

These ideals borrow from Heart of the Ozarks, a community nonprofit in Springfield, Missouri, that pairs emergency help with tools for the long run. otohay blog is not affiliated with them.

Four commitments

I label my opinions.

When a piece is my view, it says so. Opinion pieces are marked on the index.

I link my sources.

Facts link to where they come from. Uncertain parts are marked as uncertain.

I make room for disagreement.

The strongest fair objection belongs inside the essay, not outside it.

I fix my mistakes in the open.

If something turns out wrong, I correct it at the top of the post, with the date and what changed.

The shape of a question

This is the order I am building new essays around. Each step answers something a fair reader would ask.

  1. Why it mattersWhy this question is worth your time, and mine.
  2. What we knowWhat is documented, what is uncertain, and what I think, kept apart.
  3. Who decidesWho has the power to change it.
  4. Who paysWho benefits, and who bears the costs.
  5. Who gets missedWho falls through the cracks, and what it would take to reach them.
  6. The best objectionThe strongest fair case against my view.
  7. One changeA specific proposal, its trade-offs, and how we would know if it helped.
  8. Sources and correctionsEvery source, plus a dated note on anything I fixed.

What this is not

Independent and unofficial. otohay blog is a personal project. It is not affiliated with, endorsed by, or paid for by Alexandria Ocasio-Cortez, any campaign, party, or political committee. Essays are opinion. Sourced claims are linked where they appear.

Most of these ideas aren't mine. I'm passing on what I learned from my professors at San Diego State.

Everything here is education and opinion. It is not therapy, diagnosis, or treatment, and it is not a substitute for a licensed professional.

Heads up: I write bluntly. My frustration is with systems, and never with you. If a post brings up something heavy, it's okay to stop reading any time.
