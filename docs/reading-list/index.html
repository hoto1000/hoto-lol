Reading list

These are the books I'm reading to understand how our economy got this way, and what people have done to change it. I'm still learning, so read them with me and argue with them too.

I grouped them so you can pick a place to start. Six of them are also the six weeks of the reading circle, and there's a printable packet with a page for each week. Most of them are at the public library.

Start here

The Communist Manifesto

Karl Marx and Friedrich Engels, 1848. A short pamphlet that says the history of society is a history of conflict between classes. It argues that working people would one day overthrow capitalism and end private property.

Why it's here: almost every argument about capitalism still circles back to it. It's short, so you can read it yourself and decide what you think. In the reading circle it's week 2.

Toward the African Revolution

Frantz Fanon, essays from 1952 to 1961, collected in 1964. Articles, essays and letters, many first printed in the underground newspaper of Algeria's National Liberation Front. They follow his thinking on racism, colonialism and the fight for African independence.

Why it's here: Fanon treats racism and colonialism as systems that people built, which means people can take them apart. That's how I want to look at capitalism too. Start with "Racism and Culture," a speech he gave in 1956. In the reading circle it's week 1.

The Quiet Before: On the Unexpected Origins of Radical Ideas

Gal Beckerman, 2022. Beckerman argues that radical ideas usually take shape in small, private spaces where a few people can talk things through. He worries social media is crowding those spaces out.

Why it's here: it explains why slow conversations in small groups matter before any big change. That's the whole idea behind a reading circle. In the circle it's week 5.

History

How Europe Underdeveloped Africa

Walter Rodney, 1972. Rodney argues that the gap between Europe and Africa came from Europe exploiting Africa, and that colonialism left damage that lasts today.

Why it's here: it shows how one place's wealth can be tied to another place's poverty. It also asks who "development" is measured for. In the reading circle it's week 3.

The Young Lords: A Radical History

Johanna Fernández, 2020. The history of the Young Lords, poor and working-class Puerto Rican young people in Chicago and New York who modeled themselves on the Black Panther Party. Fernández shows how their protests won reforms and helped spread socialist ideas in the U.S.

Why it's here: it shows people organizing around what their own neighborhood needed. In the reading circle it's week 4.

The other side

Intellectuals and Society

Thomas Sowell, 2009 (expanded in 2011). Sowell argues that intellectuals shape policy mostly by shaping public opinion, and that they're rarely held to account when their ideas fail. He says their fixes for society have often been badly wrong.

Why it's here: a lot of his critique lands on people who think the way I do. A reading path that only agrees with itself isn't worth much, and if I can't answer his best points, I want to know that. In the reading circle it's week 6.

Fiction

Slaughterhouse-Five

Kurt Vonnegut, 1969. An antiwar novel about Billy Pilgrim, an American prisoner of war who survives the firebombing of Dresden and comes "unstuck in time." It's funny and grim at once.

Why it's here: it's about ordinary people getting used up by big systems. One line, from a made-up propaganda pamphlet in the book, says "America is the wealthiest nation on Earth, but its people are mainly poor, and poor Americans are urged to hate themselves." It isn't in the six weeks, so it works as a seventh meeting.

What I want to do next

I'm reading these slowly, and I want to keep adding to this list as I learn, especially history from closer to home. If you want to read with friends, the reading circle page has five steps and four questions, and that's enough to start.
