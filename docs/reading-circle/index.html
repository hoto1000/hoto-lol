Get organized

Start a reading circle

A lot of big movements started small, with a few people reading and talking at a kitchen table. You can start one with your friends this week. You don't need to be an expert. I'm still learning too.

Print the reading circle packet (PDF, 7 pages)

Where to start

  1. Ask 3 to 5 people you trust. Friends, classmates, coworkers, family. Small is good, because everyone gets to talk.
  2. Pick a time and a place. Once a week for an hour. A living room, a library room or a park bench all work.
  3. Read one short piece each week. A chapter or an essay. If someone didn't finish, they should still come.
  4. Talk with the same four questions. They're below, and they keep things simple.
  5. After a few weeks, pick one thing to do together. It could be a need on your block, at school or at work.

The four questions

  1. What stood out to you?
  2. Where do you see this in our lives right now?
  3. What confused you, or what do you disagree with?
  4. What's one small thing we could try?

A six-week starter list

These are books I'm reading now. Most of them are at the public library.

  1. Week 1: Frantz Fanon, "Racism and Culture," in Toward the African Revolution. A short speech from 1956.
  2. Week 2: Karl Marx and Friedrich Engels, The Communist Manifesto, Part I. It's short and still argued about, so read it and decide what you think.
  3. Week 3: Walter Rodney, How Europe Underdeveloped Africa, chapter 1. What "development" means, and who it's measured for.
  4. Week 4: Johanna Fernández, The Young Lords, any one chapter. Young people in East Harlem who started by asking neighbors what they needed.
  5. Week 5: Gal Beckerman, The Quiet Before, the introduction. Why the slow talking before a movement matters.
  6. Week 6: Thomas Sowell, Intellectuals and Society, any one chapter. A view from the other side, and good practice in hearing people you disagree with.

A few ground rules

  • Everyone gets a turn before anyone talks twice.
  • Push on ideas and stay kind to people.
  • What people share about their lives stays in the room.
  • Nobody has to agree, and nobody has to have read everything.

Then do something together
