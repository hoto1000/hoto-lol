The queue is the policy: who pays the hidden time tax?

Start with the phone call

Pick one everyday system: calling the government for benefits. I’m using Social Security’s national 800 number because it publishes its wait times, which most systems don’t.

Here is what the Social Security Administration’s own inspector general reported for fiscal year 2025 (October 2024 to September 2025). Callers who stayed on hold waited on average about 51 minutes in October 2024, and about 1 hour 40 minutes at the worst point in January 2025. By September 2025 that had dropped to 19 minutes. Callers who took a callback waited an average of about 109 minutes for it, and the callback wait peaked at 2 hours 32 minutes. Callers who gave up and hung up had waited between about 22 and 38 minutes on average, depending on the month.

That is a real improvement by the end of the year, and the agency says so. It is also worth knowing how the headline number works. The agency’s “average speed of answer” counts anyone who accepts a callback as a zero-minute wait, and it leaves out the time they wait for the call back. So the number people see is smaller than the time people spend.

Who can afford that wait

A wait is cheap if you have a desk job, a private phone and a boss who doesn’t mind. It gets expensive in other places.

If you’re paid by the hour, a 50-minute hold is 50 minutes you aren’t earning, or a call you take on break and hope doesn’t drop. Paid sick leave is a rough stand-in for whether you can step away: in March 2026, 83 percent of all civilian workers had access to it, but only 63 percent of workers in the lowest-paid quarter and 48 percent in the lowest-paid tenth did.

If you have a disability, a long hold can mean sitting, pressing buttons, or talking when that is the hard part. If you’re caring for someone, you may not get a long stretch where nobody needs you.

Annie Lowrey calls this the “time tax” in her book of that name: the hours lost to paperwork, phone calls and lines to get things people qualify for. She says the people charged the most are often the ones with the least time and money. She points to Wisconsin in spring 2020, where she says the state didn’t answer 99.5 percent of unemployment calls. Those are her words in an NPR interview, so I’m treating that number as her claim and not an audited figure.

The turn: waiting decides who gets in

My argument is that time isn’t only wasted in a queue. The way a system makes people wait quietly decides who is able to use it.

Nobody writes “people with hourly jobs may not apply.” But if the only way in is a long hold during business hours, the people who can’t take that hold are screened out, and nobody ever has to say so. The queue works like an eligibility rule that isn’t written down. That is my reading of the evidence above, and you can disagree with it.

My own experience

The numbers above come from Social Security because it publishes them. My own experience was applying for disability insurance, and I think the same pattern shows up there. The part that wore me down was the paperwork and getting my doctors to fill out their forms. I lost count of how many hoops there were.

The best way I can describe it is a check engine light that keeps coming back on. You deal with it, and then it pops up again. It’s one more form, one more thing to chase, one more thing sitting in the back of your mind. For a while I wondered if I was just being lazy.

Looking back, I don’t think that’s the right question. The system asked me to keep track of a lot of small tasks, and each one depended on someone else getting back to me.

One redesign

A real callback that works for the caller. The Social Security Administration already offers callbacks, but callers don’t get an estimated wait for them, and the average was about 109 minutes. A better version would:

  • Let the caller pick a time window (for example, 6 to 8 p.m.).
  • Say up front how long the callback is likely to take.
  • Count the callback wait in the published wait-time number.

This one change speaks to who can use the system, along with how fast it is.

Sources

  • Social Security Administration Office of the Inspector General, “Social Security Administration’s Telephone Metrics” (FY 2025 wait, callback and abandon times, and how callbacks are counted)
  • U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics, “Employee Benefits in the United States, March 2026,” Table 6, paid sick leave access by wage group
  • NPR, interview with Annie Lowrey about The Time Tax (Aug 31, 2026; “time tax” definition and the Wisconsin 99.5% claim)
