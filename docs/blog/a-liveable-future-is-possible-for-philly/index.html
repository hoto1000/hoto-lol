A liveable future is possible for Philly

What would it take for Philadelphia to be a city where working people can afford to stay, breathe and get around?

I grew up in Philadelphia and I live in San Diego now. I still follow Philly news, and most of it is about what’s broken. This post is my attempt to put the hard numbers next to the things that are working, because I think both are true at once.

What makes Philly hard to live in

Start with money. Pew’s 2026 State of the City report found Philadelphia’s poverty rate was 19.7 percent in 2024. That’s the lowest since 2000 and the first time it fell below 20 percent since 1979. It’s real progress, but it still means about one in five people in the city lives in poverty. The city’s median household income was $60,521, compared with $81,604 for the country.

Rent takes a big share of what people do earn. Pew found that 48.5 percent of Philly renters were cost-burdened in 2018 to 2022, meaning they spent 30 percent or more of their income on rent and utilities. For 2024, the Inquirer reported that almost half of the city’s renters spent 35 percent or more on housing.

Heat isn’t shared equally either. These figures come from WHYY’s report on the city’s tree plan in February 2023. At the last citywide assessment in 2018, tree canopy covered about 20 percent of Philadelphia, down about 6 percent from 2008. The poorest half of residents live in areas with about a third of the city’s street trees, and neighborhoods can differ by up to 22 degrees on hot summer days. The lowest canopy is in areas marked “hazardous” on the 1937 redlining map.

Gun violence hit a peak in 2021, with 561 homicides. And the region’s transit system spent 2025 on the edge. SEPTA cut service in August 2025, then restored it about three weeks later after the state approved up to $394 million in capital money for daily operations over two years. Neither the November 2025 state budget nor the July 2026 one included long-term transit funding. Mayor Cherelle Parker called it “unfinished business.”

None of this is an accident of weather or bad luck. In a system where housing, shade and safety go to whoever can pay, the neighborhoods with the least money get the least of all three. I’m angry about that. I also get why it happens. Cities compete for tax money, landlords chase returns, and state lawmakers from other regions don’t ride SEPTA.

What’s already working

Here’s the part I wanted to write. Philly has real, checkable things pointing somewhere better.

Violence is falling fast. Philadelphia had 222 homicides in 2025, the fewest since 1966, and 935 shooting victims, down nearly 60 percent since 2021. Early 2026 kept going, with homicides through the first three months down 51 percent from the same stretch of 2025, according to Axios. Researchers say no single thing explains it. The city has given about $60 million in grants to anti-violence groups since 2021, and a Penn-led study found that fixing lights, clearing vacant lots and caring for community gardens helped too. A Penn criminologist also credits big prosecutions aimed at the small number of people behind most violence.

Public money is fixing public spaces. The Rebuild program has put more than $500 million, paid for by the city’s beverage tax, into 72 neighborhood parks, rec centers and libraries. Places like the Kingsessing and Cobbs Creek rec centers have reopened. These are spaces anyone can use without buying anything.

There’s a plan for shade. The city’s Philly Tree Plan, released in February 2023, set a goal of 30 percent tree cover in every neighborhood within 30 years. It came out of 32 community meetings and a survey with more than 7,000 responses in eight languages. The words “every neighborhood” matter, because the goal is to close the gap between blocks.

Homes kept out of the market. In 2019, the Women’s Community Revitalization Project broke ground on 38 permanently affordable homes in Point Breeze, five for sale and 33 for rent, as part of a community land trust. A nonprofit keeps the land on a 99-year ground lease, and resale prices are capped, so the homes stay affordable for the next family too. Tenants are organizing as well. WHYY reported in 2026 that Philly’s known tenant unions grew from about five before the pandemic to upward of 20.

Workers owning the business. In 2025, City Council declared Philadelphia’s Year of the Cooperative, with 16 of 17 members signing on. The Philadelphia Area Cooperative Alliance helped write it. Worker-owners from places like Home Care Associates and Childspace Centers testified. In a co-op, the people doing the work own it and vote on how it runs.

Neighbors feeding neighbors. At least 40 community fridges run across the city, open 24 hours, according to Next City in August 2026. Volunteers stock them, and the idea is simple: share what you have, take what you need. The People’s Fridge alone has given out more than 2 million pounds of food.

The honest limits

I don’t want to oversell this. Poverty just under 20 percent is progress, and it still leaves about one in five Philadelphians in poverty. Next City reported that food insecurity in Philly has climbed back to nearly 20 percent, and that more than 32,000 fewer Philadelphians got SNAP benefits in May 2026 than in December 2025. Fridges run by volunteers can’t replace a program like SNAP.

The city can’t fix transit alone either. SEPTA needs steady money from Harrisburg, and two state budgets have come and gone without it. A tree plan needs years of funding and upkeep. A land trust with 38 homes is a model. It isn’t yet close to the scale of a city where about half of renters are stretched.

What gives me hope is that every item on the working list was built by people deciding something should be shared. A rec center, a fridge, a co-op and a tenant union all treat a need as something we meet together.

Something you can do this month

If you live in Philly, pick one thing. You could find the tenant union in your area, or start talking with neighbors in your building, using the Philly organizing page on this site as a place to start. You could also call your state representative and senator and ask them where they stand on long-term SEPTA funding. If reading is more your speed, you could start a small reading circle with a few friends and read about how we got here and what comes next.

I believe a liveable Philly is possible because pieces of it already exist. Next I want to learn how other cities have funded transit for the long term, and whether any of it could work in Pennsylvania.
