What I mean when I say capitalism

What do people actually mean when they say capitalism?

This is part one of a series I’m calling “Capitalism, explained plainly.”

Why I’m starting with a definition

The word capitalism keeps coming up on this blog. When people argue about it, I’ve noticed they’re often arguing about different things. One person means buying and selling. Another means billionaires. Another means freedom.

So before I say anything else, I want to say plainly what I mean. I’m a grad student training to be a family therapist, and I’m not an economist. That’s why I’m leaning on the original texts and on people who study this for a living.

A working definition

The Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy has a new entry on capitalism by the political philosopher Chiara Cordelli. It says many people would identify capitalism with a system where “productive assets are privately owned,” labor is hired for wages, goods are traded in markets, investment is mostly in private hands, and “production is oriented towards profit.”

Here are the four pieces I think matter most, in plain words.

1. Private ownership of the means of production

“Means of production” is the stuff you need to make things: land, buildings, machines, tools, software, the warehouse. Under capitalism, private people and companies own most of it.

That’s different from owning your phone or your clothes. When socialists talk about ownership, they mean the factory, and your toothbrush stays yours.

2. Wage labor

Most people don’t own a factory, so they make a living by selling their time for a paycheck. Karl Marx said the worker under capitalism is “free in the double sense.” He’s free to sell his ability to work, and he’s also free of, meaning without, the things he’d need to work for himself.

Marx’s point was that this setup didn’t fall from the sky. In Capital (1867) he wrote, “Nature does not produce on the one side owners of money or commodities, and on the other men possessing nothing but their own labour-power.” It came out of history, which means people made it, and people can change it.

My first big-boy job was as a behavioral interventionist. I made $25 an hour while each session cost $250 an hour. I got 10% for the direct labor of the company, and there was no world where I would meet the CEO.

3. Production for profit, through markets

Under capitalism, companies make things to sell, and the reason they make them is to end up with more money than they put in. If something doesn’t turn a profit, it usually doesn’t get made, even when people need it.

4. Capital accumulation

Profit doesn’t just sit in a drawer. It gets put back in to make more profit. Marx put it bluntly: “Accumulate, accumulate! That is Moses and the prophets!”

He also said competition “compels him to keep constantly extending his capital, in order to preserve it.” In plain words, a business that stops growing tends to get swallowed by one that keeps growing. Greed plays a part, but the pressure is also built into the game.

My mom was a retail pharmacist, a small fish in the big world of pharmaceuticals. My dad worked for a small grassroots software company.

Markets are older than capitalism

This is the part I think gets missed most. People traded long before capitalism existed. The encyclopedia entry makes the same point: each of these features, taken alone, “can be shown to have existed for a very long time.” What’s newer is putting them all together.

Marx used a simple picture to show the difference. In ordinary trade, you sell in order to buy. A farmer sells eggs to buy shoes, and the trade is done once she has the shoes.

Capital flips that around. You start with money, buy things (including people’s work), and sell what comes out to end with more money. Marx called this “buying in order to sell,” and he saw that it has no natural stopping point: “The circulation of capital has therefore no limits.”

So when someone says capitalism is just trade, I think they’re skipping the important part. Capitalism is the combination: a small group owns the means of production, most people work for wages, and the whole thing runs on profit that gets reinvested to grow.

What socialists mean by the alternative

In the Communist Manifesto (1848), Marx and Friedrich Engels wrote that their theory “may be summed up in the single sentence: Abolition of private property.” That line scares people, and I understand why.

In the same chapter, they say which property they mean. Their plan “deprives no man of the power to appropriate the products of society; all that it does is to deprive him of the power to subjugate the labour of others by means of such appropriations.” In plain words, you keep your stuff. What changes is who owns the workplaces, and whether owning one lets you live off other people’s work.

Socialists have argued for a long time about what that looks like in practice. Some mean the government owns key industries. Others mean workers own and run their own workplace together, like a worker co-op where each worker gets a vote. The goal Marx and Engels named was a society where “the free development of each is the condition for the free development of all.”

I know the word socialism scares a lot of people, including many Vietnamese families who left after 1975. I don’t want to wave that away. I want to write about that history honestly later in this series.

Socialism is a hated word. My parents were sent away from their country because of communists. I write about it because it’s what I believe in. I believe this country can be a lot better if we can heal and support the lower masses and make everything more equitable for everyone. And I mean everyone.

What defenders of capitalism argue

I want to give the other side in its own words. Adam Smith made the case in The Wealth of Nations (1776) that self-interest can end up serving other people: “It is not from the benevolence of the butcher, the brewer, or the baker that we expect our dinner, but from their regard to their own interest.” The baker wants money, I want bread, and we both walk away better off.

Milton Friedman pushed that idea further in a 1970 essay in The New York Times Magazine. He wrote that a business has one social responsibility, “to use its resources and engage in activities designed to increase its profits so long as it stays within the rules of the game.” He described those rules as open and free competition “without deception or fraud.” He also argued that in a free market “all cooperation is voluntary,” and people only join a deal if they benefit.

I think there’s something real in these arguments. Nobody has to sit in an office and plan how many loaves of bread a city needs, and the bread still shows up. Smith also wrote a whole earlier book about sympathy, which I wrote a little about in how competition wears down the people at the bottom.

Where I get stuck is the word voluntary. If you don’t own anything you can make a living with, and rent is due on the first, taking a job is voluntary in a pretty thin way. That’s the gap Marx was pointing at, and I don’t think the defenders fully answer it.

Where I’m landing for now

Here’s my working definition. Capitalism is a system where a small group privately owns what most of us need to make a living, most people work for them for wages, and production runs on profit that has to keep growing. Markets and trade are part of it, but they’re older than it, and they could exist without it.

I’m a student and I could be wrong about parts of this. If you want to try something this week, the next time the word comes up in an argument, ask the other person what they mean by capitalism before you say what you think.

Next I want to learn how this system got started, and how so many people ended up with nothing to sell but their time.

I’m a student sharing what I’m learning. This isn’t financial or legal advice.
