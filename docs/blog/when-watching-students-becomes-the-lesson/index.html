When Watching Students Becomes the Lesson

What do students learn when someone is always watching?

What do students learn when a school-issued device records what they search and write?

Student surveillance is a blunt policy with a weak case for trust. Schools are collecting more data about students than ever. A school should show a tool helps, explain what it does, and let people challenge mistakes.

The everyday practice

At 11 p.m., a ninth grader types a dark joke on a school-issued laptop. By morning, monitoring software has flagged it and an administrator has a report. Down the hall, an attendance app records three late arrivals for another student and triggers an automatic penalty. Some districts have also installed entrance cameras that match faces against a watch list.

The tools arrive through software updates, safety grants and new dashboard features, which can make surveillance seem routine. The philosopher Michel Foucault argued that modern institutions shape people less through force than through observation, records and ranking. When people do not know when they are being watched, they may start policing themselves as if observation never stops. Students learn the lesson plan, along with the sense that someone is always watching and scoring them.

This is already happening at a large scale. Gaggle says it works with about 1,500 school districts and tracks the online activity of roughly 6 million students. A U.S. Senate investigation found that more than 7,000 schools or districts used GoGuardian's monitoring products in 2021. In Vancouver, Washington, the district paid Gaggle $328,036 for three school years. The software scans what students search and write on school-issued devices around the clock, then sends anything that matches its patterns to human reviewers and, after that, to the school.

Schools should care about safety and attendance. Monitoring has expanded faster than basic questions about whether it works, whom it harms and who gets a say.

Who defines “risk,” and whose knowledge counts

Each tool defines a problem through choices: a keyword list marks danger signals, a behavior points system labels disruptive actions, and a vendor's model decides what counts as a “concerning” search.

Those choices are usually made far from the classroom, by companies and administrators. The people who have the most context are rarely asked: the teacher who knows a student was quoting a song, the parent who knows why a child is late, or the student who knows what a phrase means in their community.

When the Associated Press and the Seattle Times examined Vancouver's records, they found that from October 2023 to October 2024 nearly 2,200 students, about 10% of the district, had been the subject of an alert. The system flagged more than 1,000 documents for suicide and nearly 800 for threats of violence, and many were false alarms. At one arts high school, about 1 in 4 students had written something that triggered an alert. One student was flagged for a short story with mildly violent imagery. A keyword list had decided what that creative writing meant, without a teacher making that call.

A record can speak louder than the people who know a student. Foucault's point was that this is a form of power, even when everyone involved means well.

Who benefits, and who bears the costs

Monitoring offers easy-to-show benefits: administrators receive reports for the board, vendors get contracts, and districts can feel they have acted on safety.

The less visible costs fall unevenly:

  • Students who rely on school devices. Someone with a personal laptop can search privately at home. A student whose only computer is school-issued may still be monitored there.
  • Students already watched more closely. In a 2022 national survey by the Center for Democracy and Technology, 48% of Black students and 55% of Hispanic students said they or someone they knew had gotten in trouble because of monitoring, compared with 41% of white students.
  • Students who need help. In the same survey, 29% of LGBTQ+ students said they or someone they knew had been outed by monitoring without consent. A teenager searching for information about depression, sexuality or a family crisis may stop if they know the search could be flagged.
  • Students who were never asked. In the Vancouver records, at least six students were potentially outed to school officials after writing about being gay, transgender or struggling with gender dysphoria. In Durham, North Carolina, an advocate said a self-harm alert led to a student being outed to an unsupportive family, and the school board voted to stop using Gaggle in 2023. One Vancouver student, after learning about the monitoring, stopped searching personal health questions: “I was too scared to be curious.”
  • Students whose secrets leak. Information collected to protect students can expose them. Through a single public records request, reporters received almost 3,500 sensitive, unredacted student documents from Vancouver's monitoring system, with no password or firewall in the way. Gaggle changed its system afterward. Every record kept is also a record that could be lost.
  • Everyone's trust. Students who feel scored all day may learn to perform for the system and avoid asking honest questions.

Among teachers at schools using monitoring software, 78% said students had been flagged for discipline and 44% said a student had been contacted by law enforcement because of an alert. A flag can outlast the moment and follow a student in a file long after the situation has passed.

What the evidence and affected voices say

In some places, the public debate has already led to action. In 2020 the Lockport school district in New York switched on a $1.4 million facial recognition system. Two parents, represented by the New York Civil Liberties Union, sued, and the state paused the technology. In 2023 a state report found the risks in schools “may outweigh the benefits,” including higher false-positive rates for people of color and transgender people, and warned it “may only offer the appearance of safer schools.” That September, New York's Education Commissioner banned facial recognition in schools.

Online monitoring has not faced the same test. Independent research has not shown that this software measurably lowers student suicide or violence. A 2023 RAND study found only “scant evidence” either way and concluded that “no research to date has comprehensively examined how these programs affect youth suicide prevention.” The Vancouver officials interviewed by reporters said they do not track whether the tool works, though they believe it has saved lives. RAND published its report on Dec. 5, 2023, and says more data are needed to show how accurately AI algorithms detect suicide risk and whether the tools improve student mental health. The report itself lays out that gap.

New York's ban raises a question most communities have not asked: does monitoring make students safer, and at what price? Districts should answer it publicly, along with these questions:

  1. Which monitoring tools are in use, what they collect, and how long the data is kept.
  2. How many flags or automatic penalties were issued last year, and how many led to a real intervention.
  3. How those flags and penalties break down by student group.
  4. Whether a student or family can see what was recorded about them, and how to correct it.

Numbers are incomplete. Students, parents and teachers can describe how a flag felt, what it changed and whether anyone explained it. Their accounts belong beside vendor marketing claims.

One reform: a student data bill of rights

I would start with a school-board policy written with students, families and teachers. It could set four commitments:

  • Transparency. Publish every monitoring tool in use, what it collects and why, in plain language, every year.
  • Collect less. No monitoring outside school hours on school devices without a specific, documented reason. No facial recognition.
  • A human in the loop. A flag alone cannot trigger an automatic penalty or referral. Someone who knows the student reviews it first.
  • See it, challenge it, let it expire. Students and families can view their records, correct mistakes and appeal decisions. Minor discipline and risk notes expire after a set period, so they do not follow a student forever.

Students who need support still deserve it. A counselor can help a struggling student without making every conversation a permanent entry. Support should come with clear rules about who can see what. The default should be the least sensitive record that still lets help happen.

FERPA gives parents, and students once they turn 18, the right to inspect education records within 45 days of asking, request changes to inaccurate or misleading records, and seek a hearing if a school refuses. Few families know this. The law predates laptops that can generate an 11 p.m. report; a local policy could make these rights easier to use and clarify that monitoring flags and risk notes count as records too.

How to know if it works. After one school year, the district could report publicly on:

  • The number of flags and automatic penalties, compared with the year before.
  • How many flags led to real help and how many resulted only in a record.
  • Whether gaps between student groups narrowed.
  • How many records were viewed, corrected or expired.
  • What students and families say in a short survey about trust and fairness.

What I still don't know

The strongest case for monitoring is that it can sometimes identify a student in real danger, such as someone searching for ways to hurt themselves, and get help to them in time. The alerts are not all noise: one Oklahoma district received close to 1,000 alerts in the 2023–24 school year, 281 of them about suicide, and among the screenshots reporters reviewed was a student asking, “Why does my boyfriend hit me?”

Some of those flags point to real children in real trouble, and I take that seriously. That is why this proposal keeps human review and support, while stopping short of banning monitoring altogether. If a district's own numbers showed that its flags regularly led to real help, with little harm and no unequal impact, I would rethink how far these limits should go.

I still want to know how often an alert leads to a response a student experiences as helpful, and how often it leaves only a record behind. What would change if students and families could see and challenge a flag before it shaped a school decision?

What students learn

Schools teach more than subjects. When students are watched and scored all day, they may learn that a record matters more than they do. Seeing, questioning and helping write the rules can teach them that institutions must answer to the people they serve.

If your school uses monitoring software, bring the four questions above to your next board meeting. I want to know whether the answers lead to changes students can actually see.
