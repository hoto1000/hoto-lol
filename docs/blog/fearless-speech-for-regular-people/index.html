What does it cost you to say the true thing, and is it worth it?

Fearless speech for regular people: parrhesia meets boundary scripts

Oct 28, 2026 · 5 min read

Ideas from Michel Foucault

Foucault’s last lectures gave a name to a kind of courage that has little to do with shouting. I have been learning how much risk and honesty are part of what he meant.

He called it parrhesia

The word comes from ancient Greek and means roughly “saying everything,” or speaking the whole truth out loud. Foucault followed the idea through Euripides, the Cynics, and the Roman Stoics in two lecture courses, The Government of Self and Others (1983) and The Courage of Truth (1984), as well as in Berkeley talks later collected as Fearless Speech.

I understand his definition as having three parts, and all three need to be there.

First, the speaker believes what they say. The words come from their own conviction, not from a talking point someone handed them.

Second, speaking carries a risk. A person might lose status, a job, a relationship, or someone's good opinion. When speaking costs nothing, Foucault would not call it parrhesia. He was blunt about the parrhesiastes risking the relationship itself in order to tell the truth.

Third, the truth is spoken to someone who could hurt the speaker, usually a person with more power. Sometimes that person is a friend. That can sound easier, though it often isn't.

I take parrhesia to describe a moment more than a personality trait: someone with less power says what they believe is true and accepts what follows.

What it is not

Venting releases pressure and can bring relief. Parrhesia is directed toward being heard by the person in front of you. I see those as different aims.

Bluntness alone doesn't make speech parrhesia. It can be a way of speaking quickly, without asking whether plain words are also being said well.

Criticism from a person who holds power doesn't fit Foucault's account. He made this point carefully. A boss dressing down an employee has little at stake, so that speech isn't fearless. Power can make speaking easy, which moves the situation away from what Foucault described.

Oversharing has a different shape too. Parrhesia is one true sentence, directed to a particular person, with something real at stake.

What Foucault cared about most

Foucault connected parrhesia with caring for yourself. In the ancient world, telling the truth to a friend was considered a service. A friend was expected to speak plainly about your faults, and you were expected to take it. I understand the practice as two people trying to help each other live better through honesty.

Foucault also connected parrhesia to how a person relates to themself. My own reading is that speaking when it costs you can show you what you actually believe. I don't mean that as a claim from Foucault.

For me, the return is a clearer sense of where you stand.

The honest caveat

Speaking up carries costs, and people do not carry them equally. If your job, safety, visa, housing, or physical wellbeing depends on someone, that matters and should shape the decision. Nobody owes anyone fearless speech. Foucault described a rare, risky act, not a moral obligation.

I find it more useful to name the true thing, understand its cost, and choose deliberately. Sometimes that choice is silence. It can mean a person is reading the room accurately, and it isn't cowardice.

What changes it from an outburst into a boundary

I understand a boundary as a sentence about your own actions. It says what you will do and leaves the other person outside the role of deciding who they must be.

These four examples show ways to put that into words.

At work, when a request keeps expanding
“I can take this on, but I need to drop one of the other two projects to do it well. Which one should come off my plate?”

The sentence names a limit without accusing anyone, and gives the tradeoff back to the person who created it. That connection between honesty and the limit is what I notice.

With a manager who moves the goalposts
“I want to hit the target you have in mind. Can we put the current version in writing so I am working against the same thing you are?”

This asks for a shared reference point without starting a confrontation. The written version can also protect the person asking for clarity.

With family, when a topic keeps coming back
“I love you and I am not going to talk about this at dinner. I will happily talk about anything else.”

The sentence is short and warm, and it doesn't turn the conversation into a case against the other person. It states what you will do without asking them to change their personality.

When someone asks you to explain a decision you have already made
“I have thought about it, and my answer is settled. I know you disagree, and I am okay with that.”

This can be the hardest example because it doesn't take on the task of making the other person comfortable with your choice. That is part of what makes the boundary clear.

Across all four, I see a similar pattern. Say what is true, keep the sentence about your own action, and avoid over-explaining because that can invite negotiation. The discomfort may still be there, and the examples leave room for it to sit without rushing to fix it.

One thing to try this week

I could start with one thing I have been rehearsing in my head for weeks. It doesn't need to be the biggest issue or the one that could put a job at risk. It can be something small and real.

Before saying it, write the sentence down. Keep it under two lines and make it about what you will do, not what the other person should be.

Then say it once, at a normal volume, without following it with a paragraph of reasons.

Afterward, I want to notice whether I still believe what I said now that it is out of my head. That is the information parrhesia can give me.
