What I'm Learning About Third Parties

Why do Americans want a third party but almost never vote for one?

Why do so many people say they want a third party but vote for one of the two major parties? Most Americans say they want a third major party. Almost none vote for one.

My working answer is that third parties get boxed out by the rules. I started reading because I wanted to understand that gap. Here is what I found, with the sources at the bottom and the uncertain parts marked.

The more I read, the more the rules looked like the main issue. Third parties in the United States lose for structural reasons. Their ideas and voters face a system that favors two parties.

Those structures can be changed, and a few states have already changed them. Until they change, the most useful thing a third party can do is make the big two steal from it.

I care because people feel shut out. Gallup has found for years that more Americans call themselves independents than Democrats or Republicans. In its Governance poll conducted September 1 to 23, 2023, Gallup found that 63 percent said the two parties did such a poor job that a third major party was needed, the highest share since Gallup first asked in 2003.

That is a lot of people who feel unrepresented by the only two doors they are allowed to walk through. Feeling unrepresented is bad for a country and, in my experience, bad for the people in it. The Gallup article also says the answer may register frustration with the two-party system, so it cannot tell us whether those respondents would support a particular new party or candidate.

The wall, brick by brick

The evidence points to at least five obstacles facing third parties in America. Any one could block a new party.

  • Winner takes all. Almost every US election awards the seat to whoever gets the most votes in a single district. A party that wins 20 percent everywhere wins nothing anywhere. In 1992, Ross Perot took nearly 19 percent of the national vote, the best independent showing in eighty years, and received zero electoral votes. In the 1950s, French political scientist Maurice Duverger noticed that this kind of system almost always settles into two parties, because voters learn not to waste a ballot. People call this Duverger's law. The label sounds absolute. The pattern is a strong habit, and it holds.
  • Getting on the ballot. Democrats and Republicans are usually on the ballot automatically. Other candidates have to petition state by state, across fifty different sets of rules for signatures, deadlines, and fees. Ballotpedia tracks those requirements, which range from a few thousand signatures to well over a hundred thousand in the biggest states. A new party can spend most of its money and volunteers just earning the right to lose.
  • Sore-loser laws. In most states, losing a party primary means you cannot run as an independent in the general election. That shuts off the most obvious path for a popular figure who falls out with their party.
  • The debate stage. Starting in 2000, the Commission on Presidential Debates required 15 percent in national polls to qualify for the stage. Perot got in during 1992 and was kept out in 1996. Nobody outside the two parties has cleared that bar since. In 2024, the campaigns went around the commission and arranged debates directly with television networks. The terms were similar, so the door stayed shut.
  • Money and the spoiler fear. Federal matching funds for the general election go to parties that got at least 5 percent last time, which almost none do. Donors and voters also worry that a vote for a third candidate helps the major candidate they like least. Whether that fear is justified in any given race is a real question. Florida is one place to look.

About the spoiler argument

In 2000, Ralph Nader won around 97,000 votes in Florida. George W. Bush carried the state, and with it the presidency, by 537. Democrats have not forgiven him. I cannot know how Nader's voters would have behaved without him.

Studies of exit polls suggest a chunk would have stayed home, while the rest would have split unevenly toward Gore. That probably still flips Florida. The word "probably" is doing some work there.

The honest version is that spoiling is real and rarer than the fear of it. The fear does most of the damage on its own. It scares people out of voting for what they actually want.

What third parties have done anyway

The historian Richard Hofstadter wrote that third parties are like bees: once they have stung, they die. The sting is the point. The Populists of the 1890s demanded a graduated income tax and the direct election of senators. Both are now in the Constitution, and the Populists are gone.

Eugene Debs ran as a Socialist five times, reached 6 percent in 1912, and lived to see the big parties adopt pieces of his platform. Theodore Roosevelt's Progressives took 27 percent that same year and pushed both parties leftward for a generation. The Republican Party began as a third party in 1854, replaced the Whigs within six years, and is the only one ever to do that.

More recent wins have been smaller and more local. Jesse Ventura was governor of Minnesota on the Reform Party line. Angus King and Bill Walker were independent governors of Maine and Alaska.

In Nebraska in 2024, Dan Osborn, an independent mechanic, ran for Senate with no party and lost by about seven points in a state Trump won by twenty. He ran again in 2026, and this time the Democratic nominee dropped out to clear his path. He lost again, by about seven points in early unofficial results, to Pete Ricketts. Democrats did the same in Idaho and South Dakota.

The 2026 candidates page has all of it. This is a new pattern. I do not know yet whether it is a fluke or a strategy.

The structures that are already changing

Three reforms get most of the attention. Each one already exists somewhere in the country.

  • Ranked-choice voting. Voters rank candidates, starting with their first choice. If that candidate is eliminated, the vote moves to the second choice. The spoiler problem mostly disappears. Maine uses it for federal elections, and Alaska uses a top-four primary with a ranked general election. Voters in Alaska narrowly kept the system in a 2024 repeal vote, and a second repeal was on the ballot again in 2026. On the other side, nineteen states have banned it, mostly with the argument that it confuses voters and slows counting.
  • Fusion voting. A small party nominates the same candidate as a major party, and votes cast on both lines are added together. The candidate sees exactly how many votes the small party delivered and owes it something. New York and Connecticut have used it for a century, New Hampshire allows it too, and Oregon allows a partial version. On the 2026 ballots, Jeff Merkley and Ned Lamont both ran on the Working Families line, and both won in early unofficial results. Fusion voting was common in the 1800s. After the Populists used it to team up with Democrats in the 1890s, Republican lawmakers started rolling it back in the states. That suggests they thought it worked.
  • Proportional representation. Multi-member districts let a party with 20 percent of the vote win about 20 percent of the seats. This is how most democracies elect their legislatures. A 1967 federal law requires single-member House districts, so changing that rule would take an act of Congress. That makes this the least likely of the three. A bill to do it, the Fair Representation Act, has been introduced and has not moved.

Who could be affected, and what might I be missing

The strongest counterpoint I found is that the two big parties are already coalitions. The Democratic Party contains people who would be three parties in Europe, and so does the Republican Party. Primaries are where those factions fight.

A motivated faction can capture a whole party, as Donald Trump showed in 2016 and Bernie Sanders nearly showed twice. That view makes primary challenges look like the better tool for change. The energy, on that view, belongs inside the parties.

Countries with proportional systems offer another fair warning. More parties can mean coalitions that take months to form and fall apart in a year. Israel, Belgium, and the Netherlands have all had long stretches without a functioning government. Two parties make for a crude system, though it usually produces a winner by morning.

People bear the costs of badly designed reforms. Reformers do not. Confusing ballots fall hardest on first-time voters, older voters, and voters working in a second language. Any change should be judged by how it treats them.

What I still don't know

Would more parties change representation without making coalition-building so unstable that voters lose trust? Which reforms can make votes feel more meaningful while keeping ballots accessible to people with different levels of time, language access, and election experience?

One step I want to take

Before the next election, I want to find out whether my state allows fusion or ranked-choice voting. If there is a ballot measure or a bill on either one, I want to learn who is behind it and who is against it. You can check your state too.

Start small: look at the "also on the ballot" column for your state on the candidates page, choose one name you have never heard of, and spend ten minutes finding out what they want. Most of them will never win. I still want to know what they are asking for.

Third parties lose largely because the rules were written by the people they are running against.

Check it yourself

Gallup's poll is linked above. The National Archives Electoral College results page lets you check official outcomes, candidates, and election notes that include third-party candidates. The FEC election results page explains that historical federal results are compiled from state and territorial election offices, and where to check current certified results.

I want to keep checking which rules my state uses and whose voices the changes would make easier to hear.
