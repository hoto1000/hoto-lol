This is the script from my video series “Things therapy school taught me about society.” I wrote it with AI help from published sources, checked the facts, and recorded it in my own voice. What I said on camera may differ a little.

Where attachment theory came from

Before you could even talk, you were already learning if people would show up for you. Therapy school taught me how much that follows us, and how much society shapes it.

I’m a therapy grad student, and I’m still learning. So take this as me thinking out loud.

After World War II, the World Health Organization asked a British psychiatrist named John Bowlby to study children who had lost their homes and families. His 1951 report helped start what we now call attachment theory. The idea is that kids need at least one warm, steady person to feel safe with.

Later, psychologist Mary Ainsworth in Baltimore built a lab test called the Strange Situation. A parent leaves the room for a few minutes, then comes back. In her early studies, about 7 in 10 babies were upset when the parent left and easy to comfort when they came back. She called that secure.

Researchers call that parent a secure base. It’s the person you explore the world from, and come back to when you’re scared.

How adults love

In 1987, Cindy Hazan and Phillip Shaver put a love quiz in a newspaper. About 620 people answered, and the same kinds of patterns showed up in how adults love. Some feel safe getting close.

Some keep their distance. Some worry a lot about being left.

It can change

The part that gives me hope is that it can change. In one study that followed people from birth, some kids who weren’t secure as babies grew up secure. Researchers call it earned security. Their relationships as young adults looked about as healthy as people who were secure all along.

More than mothers

Here’s what frustrates me. Bowlby’s early work put almost all of it on mothers. Later researchers like Michael Rutter pushed back, and feminist critics pointed out that kids are raised by a lot of people.

Bowlby himself wrote, “If a community values its children it must cherish its parents.” To me that means a parent who’s stressed and alone needs support too.

Being a steady person

Here are three things I’m trying.

One. When I get close to someone, I notice my pattern. Do I pull away, or hold on tighter?

Two. I try to be a steady person for someone, in small ways, like showing up when I said I would.

Three. I check on the parents and caregivers around me, and ask what would actually help.
