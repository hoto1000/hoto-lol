What therapy ethics codes get right and miss about culture

Oct 27, 2026 · 3 min read

  3. Why it matters in practice

  5. Where I'm going with this

Every therapist I'll learn from follows a code of ethics. For my program, that means the AAMFT Code of Ethics (2015) and the CAMFT Code of Ethics (2020). For a class, I read both through a multicultural lens, and I came away with two thoughts that don't fit together easily. The codes do a lot right. They also leave some big things out.

What the codes get right

Both codes ask therapists to respect clients and treat them fairly. The AAMFT code names diversity and inclusion as part of competent practice. It tells therapists not to discriminate and to get extra education or supervision when they work with people outside their own experience. It also touches on social responsibility, which says to me that ethics reaches past one therapy room.

The CAMFT code asks therapists to notice how their own values and experiences shape their work. It also treats continuing education and self-awareness as part of doing the job well. Reading them side by side, I could see both groups trying to build cultural awareness into the rules.

Where I think they stop short

The AAMFT code talks about culture mostly in terms of competence. That wording can suggest culture is something a therapist masters and then checks off. I don't think that's how it works. I'm Vietnamese American, and I can tell you my own family still teaches me new things about my own culture.

Neither code says much about power, privilege, or how unfair systems affect mental health and the therapy relationship. The CAMFT code mentions diversity, but the language stays broad. As far as I could find, it doesn't address intersectionality directly. That gap makes it easier for a therapist to stay "neutral" or color-blind, and that can brush past what a client has actually lived through.

Why it matters in practice

The codes set a solid floor. They remind us to treat every client with dignity and fairness no matter who they are. After that, a lot depends on each therapist's own judgment and self-awareness.

That works well for therapists who already reflect on their assumptions. It might not be enough for someone who hasn't started. I hadn't thought about it this way until this assignment, but a code that depends on personal reflection only protects clients as much as the therapist's reflection goes.

A bigger frame

Both codes seem to come from a mostly Western, individual-focused way of thinking. They explain the expectation to respect differences well. They say less about how systems shape what clients go through before they ever walk in. Culture also affects trust, communication, and what healing means, and I'd like to see that spelled out more.

Where I'm going with this

I'm a student, and I'm sure I'm missing parts of the picture. I want to keep asking where a rule stops and my own responsibility starts. That means reflecting on my own culture and assumptions, and asking clients about theirs instead of guessing.

Sources: American Association for Marriage and Family Therapy. (2015). AAMFT code of ethics. California Association of Marriage and Family Therapists. (2020). CAMFT code of ethics.
