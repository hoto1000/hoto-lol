Where power and identity cross: intersectionality through Foucault

Who decides which boxes a person gets sorted into?

A reading of Kimberlé Crenshaw's idea of intersectionality next to the work of Michel Foucault. Foucault died in 1984 and never used the word intersectionality. Putting the two together is my own reading, and I am not speaking for either of them.

On this blog I've written about social location and privilege. Both posts said the parts of who we are stack. Race, class, gender, language and immigration story pile on top of each other.

Those posts left me with a question I didn't answer. If the parts stack, who does the stacking, and where? This post is my attempt to work through it with two thinkers who never met.

Where the word comes from

Kimberlé Crenshaw is a law professor. She used the word intersectionality in a 1989 paper in the University of Chicago Legal Forum. She was building on Black feminist writers who came before her, including the Combahee River Collective, whose 1977 statement described oppressions as "interlocking."

One of the cases in her paper was DeGraffenreid v. General Motors, decided in 1976. Five Black women said GM's seniority system pushed them out during layoffs, because the company had not hired Black women before 1964. The court said they could bring a race claim or a sex claim, but would not let them combine the two.

GM had hired women before 1964. They were white women. So on the court's view, there was no sex discrimination, and the race claim was treated as a separate matter. The thing that actually happened to these women had no box to go in.

That makes me angry. Five women walked into court with a real loss, and the law's own categories were the reason they walked out with nothing.

Crenshaw's point was that some harm only shows up where the categories cross. If you look at one category at a time, you can miss it completely.

What Foucault looked at

Foucault spent much of his work on how power runs through ordinary institutions. In Discipline and Punish (1975), he wrote about prisons, schools, hospitals and the army. He showed how watching people, testing them, ranking them and keeping records on them trains them to act a certain way.

He also tied power to knowledge. Whoever gets to define what counts as normal, healthy or reasonable holds a kind of power. Over time, those definitions start to look like plain facts about people.

In The History of Sexuality, Volume 1 (1976), he argued that power comes from everywhere. It moves through many small relationships, in families, clinics and workplaces as well as governments. He also wrote that where there is power, there is resistance.

In that same book he described what he called biopower. Modern states manage whole populations through numbers like birth rates, health records and life expectancy. Counting people is one way of governing them.

Putting them side by side

Here is where the two ideas connect for me. Crenshaw shows that the categories stack. Foucault helps me ask where the categories come from and how they get kept in place.

The court in DeGraffenreid was using the categories the law already had. Race claim. Sex claim.

The court treated those boxes as if they described how people actually live. Because the knowledge was built that way, the women's experience didn't register as real.

I think the same thing can happen with numbers. If a report breaks data down by race, then by gender, then by income, one at a time, a group sitting where those cross can disappear. Nobody has to intend that. The form does it.

I also understand why systems work this way. A court has to sort claims somehow, and an office has to count people somehow. One category at a time is easier to run and easier to check. The cost is that what's simple for the system can leave a person unseen.

Where the hope is

Foucault's line about resistance is the part I hold onto. Power runs through ordinary places, and so does pushback. The women in DeGraffenreid lost their case, but their case is a big part of why Crenshaw gave this problem a name.

The Combahee River Collective were a small group of Black women in Boston who met and wrote together. Their 1977 statement still gets read today. Good keeps showing up in places like that, usually from people nobody put in charge.

There are practical fixes too. A form can leave room for someone to describe their own life in their own words. A report can look at where groups cross, like Black women or older immigrants, as well as each group alone. A person in a helping job can ask one more question before deciding they understand.

The strongest objection

Some critics argue that Foucault's picture of power is so spread out that it's hard to say who is responsible for anything. The philosopher Nancy Fraser made a version of this argument in 1981. She said Foucault describes power well but makes it hard to say which uses of power are wrong.

Crenshaw's work points at specific harms, specific courts and specific laws. So there is a real tension in reading them together. I don't think Foucault's ideas should be used to blur who made a decision. In DeGraffenreid, a real court made a real ruling.

Where I got it wrong

In my post on social location, I wrote about a training role-play where I almost handed a client the advice I wished someone had given me. Her family sounded like mine. I stopped myself, but only just.

Reading Crenshaw and Foucault together, I see one more piece of that moment. The categories I shared with her felt like knowledge about her. That feeling of already knowing is exactly where I need to slow down.

What I still don't know

How does a therapist ask about the parts of someone's life that cross, without turning the conversation into a checklist of labels? Which intake forms in my field ask about one category at a time, and what do they miss? How do I hold a pattern in mind while letting the person in front of me lead?

Where I'm going with this

I want to practice noticing when a form, a framework or my own first guess is sorting someone one category at a time. When I catch it, I want to ask what sits where those categories cross, and let the person tell me.
