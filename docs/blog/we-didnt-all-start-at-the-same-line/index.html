Social privilege: we didn't all start at the same line

Last fall, one of my classes had us watch a video called What Is Privilege? A group of people stood side by side in one straight line. Someone read statements out loud, and people stepped forward or back when each one was true for them.

Everyone started even. By the end, they were spread out all over the place. Nobody in that line had picked where they ended up. They were answering questions about lives they were born into.

The statement that got me

Most statements were ones I expected, about money, race, and safety. Then one came up about growing up with both parents in the home. I had never thought of that as privilege. It was just my normal. Two parents can mean more stability, more support, more hands, and more money. That foundation can shape a child's life, and almost nobody counts it when they talk about who "earned" what.

That doesn't mean a home with one parent is missing love. Plenty of single parents hold everything together and raise kids who feel deeply cared for. The privilege is in the extra time and resources, not in how much a family loves each other.

That was the moment privilege stopped feeling like a word other people needed to think about.

In its September 2025 report, the Census Bureau lists 2024 official poverty rates of 4.3% for married-couple families and 21.8% for families with a female householder and no spouse present. These family-level rates do not explain the difference or measure an individual child's support. See Table A-2.

Where I'd be standing

Watching people move forward and backward made me uncomfortable. Knowing inequality exists is one thing. Seeing it laid out among real people made it hard to look away from.

So I tried to figure out where I'd be standing. I'm Vietnamese American. I've had real advantages, like access to education. I've also dealt with cultural stereotypes and felt out of place in rooms where nobody looked like me.

I'd probably be somewhere in the middle, stepping forward on some questions and back on others. Privilege comes in layers. Most people have some of it and lack some of it, and which layer matters depends on the room you're in.

The effort story

Here's the blunt part. A lot of people believe success only comes down to effort. Work hard and you'll make it. If you didn't make it, you didn't work hard enough.

Effort matters. I believe that. The strongest counterargument is that effort and choices shape outcomes, and people can change their starting conditions. A line exercise cannot measure anyone's effort or predict their future.

But that story skips over the starting line, and that's where prejudice sneaks in. Once you believe everyone started even, the people behind you start to look lazy. You end up blaming them for barriers they never built and can't always see past.

Why this matters to me

I'm training to be a therapist, and clients will come from every kind of background. Without thinking about privilege, I could miss big parts of their story.

If a client struggles and my answer is basically "just work harder," without noticing the systems pushing against them, I could do real harm. I'd be asking them to fit my standard when my job is to meet them where they are.

The same goes outside therapy. It's easy to assume everyone has the same chances as you. I need to ask what someone might be up against.

It's not about guilt

The video also reminded me of something I needed to hear. Privilege isn't about feeling guilty. I didn't choose the circumstances I was born into, and neither did you.

What I do get to choose is how I respond. I can use the advantages I have to understand other people better, question my own assumptions, and show some empathy before I judge.

Try your own line

Grab a piece of paper. For each statement that's true for you, make a tally mark. Nobody is grading this, and there's no good or bad score.

  1. I grew up with both parents in the home.
  2. My family never worried about keeping the lights on.
  3. The language spoken at home matched the language at school.
  4. I saw people who looked like me in my textbooks and on TV.
  5. My family could help me pay for college.
  6. I've never been followed around a store.
  7. I've never had to wonder if a building would be accessible to me.

Now count your marks. Whatever your number is, it says something about where you started, not about what you're worth. If your count was high, it might be worth thinking about how different the same questions would feel for someone with a lower one.

What I still don't know

What fits inside a yes-or-no answer like “I grew up with both parents”? How can I ask about someone's starting point without treating it as their whole story?

Where I'm going with this

I want to keep building this awareness, because it's easy to lose. Next time I catch myself thinking someone just needs to try harder, I want to ask what their line looked like first.
