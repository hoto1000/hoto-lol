Who decides what counts as normal?

When a label becomes the problem: power/knowledge meets diagnosis

Oct 27, 2026 · 4 min read

Ideas from Michel Foucault

Think about the last form that asked you to describe yourself in checkboxes. Good, fair, poor. Normal, elevated, high. The form assumes someone already decided where those lines fall.

Foucault's early work on madness

Foucault's first major book, History of Madness (1961), follows one category as it changes shape across centuries. In the Renaissance, madness had a kind of voice. It was strange, unsettling, and sometimes treated as a form of insight. Then came the great confinement in the 1600s, when the mad were locked away alongside the poor and the idle. Then came medicine, which turned madness into a condition to be studied, diagnosed, and treated.

His point was that “madness” was no fixed object waiting to be discovered, though he never claimed that nothing was ever wrong with anyone. It was a category that kept being redrawn by the institutions doing the confining. The category did real work. It sorted who got heard, who got locked up, and who got to define the terms.

The medical gaze

The Birth of the Clinic (1963) adds another piece. Foucault describes a shift in how medicine looks at people. The patient stops being a person with a story and becomes a case, a body to be read, a set of signs to be classified. He called it the medical gaze.

That gaze serves a purpose, since it is how doctors learn to see patterns fast enough to actually help. But a gaze is still a way of looking, and every way of looking has blind spots. What does not fit the pattern can get lost.

Discipline and the norm

Discipline and Punish (1975) brings it home. I covered the panopticon in an earlier post, and the other half of that book is about norms.

Foucault argued that modern institutions do not just punish what is wrong. They produce a standard of what is normal, then measure everyone against it. Schools rank. Clinics classify. Workplaces evaluate. Over time, the measurement starts to feel like a plain description of reality, and the decision behind it fades from view.

That is the bridge to the label.

Power/knowledge

This is the concept that ties it together. Foucault did not treat knowledge as neutral information sitting in a library. He argued knowledge and power are produced together. Whoever has the authority to name something also shapes how it will be seen, studied, and treated.

A diagnosis is exactly this. It is knowledge. It is also an exercise of power, in the plain sense that one person with credentials gets to decide what to call what another person is living through.

The part people get wrong

Foucault is often read as saying medicine is a lie or that diagnosis is oppression. That reading misses his argument and helps nobody.

Diagnoses can be lifesaving. They can open doors to care, insurance, accommodations, and community. They can give someone a word for something they carried alone for years. Having a name for a pattern is often a relief.

His point is narrower. Labels are made by people, in history, for reasons. Since they are made, it is fair to ask what a label does as well as whether it is accurate.

When the label becomes the self

The risk is a quiet one. Once a category exists, it can start to swallow the person inside it.

You are not only a diagnosis. But when every appointment, form, and conversation routes through the label, it becomes easy to narrate your whole life in its language. Foucault worried that sorting is so smooth you stop noticing it happening, whether or not you were sorted correctly.

There is a second effect too. Norms sort people into normal and not, and landing on the wrong side of that line carries real weight in how others treat you. That weight is social, not medical, and it deserves its own name.

So what do you do with this

Keep the care and ask better questions.

When a label lands, a useful question is “who decided this is where the line goes, what was that decision built on, and what does the label do to how I see myself?” You can hold a diagnosis as useful and still keep a little distance from it.

You can also notice the parts of you the category does not cover. A category is a map, and maps leave out most of the territory.

One thing to try this week

Take one label you carry. A diagnosis, a type, a personality result, whatever sits in your file. Write down two lists.

First, what the label has given you. Access, words, treatment, understanding, community.

Second, what it has never described. The parts of you that do not fit, the days it does not explain, the things you would still be if the label vanished tomorrow.

Then read the second list again. That is the part the norm was never built to hold.
