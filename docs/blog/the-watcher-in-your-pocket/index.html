Do you act differently when you think someone might be watching?

The watcher in your pocket: the panopticon meets social media

Oct 26, 2026 · 4 min read

Ideas from Michel Foucault, Jeremy Bentham

In 1786, an English philosopher named Jeremy Bentham visited his brother Samuel in Russia and sketched a prison he called the Panopticon. The basic idea came from Samuel, and Jeremy turned it into a prison.

The design was a ring of cells around a central tower. Each cell was lit and open to the tower, where one guard could look into any cell at any moment. The prisoners could never tell when they were being watched.

What makes the design clever, and cruel, is that the guard did not have to be there. Once people believed they might be seen, they began policing themselves. Bentham called it a “new mode of obtaining power of mind over mind.”

Foucault's point

Almost two hundred years later, Michel Foucault took up the design in Discipline and Punish (1975) and used it to make a much larger argument.

He was asking how modern power works. His answer was that it works more through discipline than through violence or public spectacle. Schools, barracks, hospitals, factories, and prisons share a basic set of tools: timetables, exams, ranks, files, and constant visibility.

The Panopticon became a clear symbol for that argument. Visibility can turn into a trap. You cannot tell when the eye is on you, so you act as if it is always there. Foucault described the inmate this way: “is seen, but he does not see; he is the object of information, never a subject in communication.”

The change happens inside the person. Power shifts from something done to you into something you do to yourself. Foucault called the result a docile body. People had learned to manage their own behavior ahead of time, without anyone having to break them.

Foucault also wrote about the norms that discipline produces. It sorts people into normal and abnormal, healthy and unhealthy, employable and not. Those categories can feel like plain facts about the world, even when they come from an institution's decision about what to measure.

So what does this have to do with your phone

A social-media feed has a shape that reminded me of the Panopticon. You are not in a cell, and there is no tower, though the comparison can still be useful.

Your profile is like the cell. It is lit, legible, and there to be seen. Your audience takes the place of the tower. You cannot see who is looking or when. The number on a post gives a rough proxy for attention, though it never tells you who the eye belongs to.

Algorithms do more than watch. They rank, sort, and predict. They decide whose post travels and whose disappears. Measurement helps determine what gets noticed, which connects with the norm-making Foucault described.

Then comes the part Foucault might recognize most: you start doing the work for them.

You might draft a caption three times, check the post again at 11pm, or leave out a strange or unfinished thought because it will not perform. Nobody forced those choices. You made them after taking in the possibility of being watched.

That is the loop: self-censorship from within, with no censor above.

Two honest caveats

The comparison has limits. Bentham's guard had authority, and your audience mostly does not. Likes are not a prison sentence. Foucault wrote about institutions with real power to confine, classify, and treat people. The image works as a comparison, and it should not be read as a diagnosis of your feed.

Foucault's writing does not offer five steps to a happier life. He wrote about how power circulates and how people can push back in small, specific ways. In his framing, critique is the art of not being governed quite so much.

That last idea feels useful here. You cannot log out of power. I want to keep in view that you can notice where the gaze has moved from the tower into your own head, then decide how much of your life it gets to author.

One thing to try this week

Choose one thing you would have posted and leave it unposted. Say it to a friend, write it in a notebook, or leave it in your head. This is a small experiment, not a punishment or a decision to quit social media. Notice how much attention goes into anticipating the reaction, and see what happens to the sentence when nobody is grading it.

If that feels too small, try the reverse. Post something deliberately unoptimized. No hook, no hashtags, no timing strategy. See if the sky falls.

As you scroll, ask one more question: who is measuring what? Every feed makes judgments about what deserves to be seen. You are allowed to have opinions about the judge.
