The watcher in your pocket: the panopticon meets social media

Do you act differently when you think someone might be watching?

Oct 26, 2026 · 5 min read

What does my phone have to do with Jeremy Bentham's Panopticon? I think it can feel like a pocket-sized panopticon, with serious limits.

In 1786, English philosopher Jeremy Bentham visited his brother Samuel in Russia. The basic idea came from Samuel. Jeremy sketched a prison called the Panopticon.

Cells formed a ring around a central tower. Each cell was lit and open to the tower. One guard could look into any cell at any moment. Prisoners could never tell when they were being watched.

That is the design's sharp trick, and its cruelty. The guard did not have to be there. People who believed they might be seen began policing themselves. Bentham called it a “new mode of obtaining power of mind over mind.”

Foucault's point

Almost two hundred years later, Michel Foucault took up the design in Discipline and Punish (1975), using it to make a larger argument about power. He argued that modern power often works through discipline, not only violence or public spectacle. Schools, barracks, hospitals, factories, and prisons share tools: timetables, exams, ranks, files, and constant visibility.

The Panopticon became a symbol for that argument. You cannot tell when the eye is on you, so you act as if it is always there. Foucault described the inmate this way: “is seen, but he does not see; he is the object of information, never a subject in communication.”

Power moves from something done to you into something you do to yourself. People learn to manage their behavior in advance, without anyone having to break them. Foucault called the result a docile body. He also wrote about norms that sort people as normal and abnormal, healthy and unhealthy, employable and not. Those categories can feel like facts, even when they come from an institution's decision about what to measure.

So what does this have to do with your phone

A social-media feed reminds me of the Panopticon. You are not in a cell, and there is no tower. The comparison can still be useful.

Your profile is like the cell: lit, legible, and there to be seen. Your audience takes the place of the tower. You cannot see who is looking or when. A post's number offers a rough proxy for attention, though it never tells you who the eye belongs to.

Algorithms do more than watch. They rank and predict. Instagram's May 31, 2023 explanation says different parts of the app use separate ranking systems, and Feed ranking draws on thousands of signals. That is the company's account, not an outside audit. Instagram's explanation of how ranking works describes how posts are surfaced. These systems decide whose post travels and whose disappears, which connects to Foucault's point about what gets noticed.

Then comes the part Foucault might recognize most: you start doing the work for them.

You might draft a caption three times, check the post again at 11pm, or leave out a strange or unfinished thought because it will not perform. Nobody forced those choices. You made them after taking in the possibility of being watched.

That is the loop: self-censorship from within, with no censor above.

Two honest caveats

The strongest counterargument is that ranking can make a huge feed useful, and many posts reach friends and followers who chose to connect. Instagram points to Following and Favorites as controls. Those choices matter, though they do not explain every post's reach.

The comparison has limits. Bentham's guard had authority; your audience mostly does not. Likes carry no prison sentence. Foucault wrote about institutions with power to confine, classify, and treat people. Use the image as a comparison, not a diagnosis of your feed.

Foucault did not offer five steps to a happier life. He wrote about how power circulates and how people push back. In his framing, critique is the art of not being governed quite so much.

That last idea feels useful here. You cannot log out of power. I want to keep in view where the gaze has moved from the tower into my own head. Then I can decide how much of my life it gets to author.

One thing to try this week

Choose one thing you would have posted and leave it unposted. Say it to a friend, write it in a notebook, or leave it in your head. This is a small experiment, not a punishment or a decision to quit social media. Notice how much attention goes into anticipating the reaction. See what happens to the sentence when nobody is grading it.

If that feels too small, try the reverse. Post something deliberately unoptimized. No hook, no hashtags, no timing strategy. See if the sky falls.

As you scroll, ask one more question: who is measuring what? Every feed makes judgments about what deserves to be seen. You are allowed to have opinions about the judge.

What I still don't know

How much of my self-editing is for people I know, and how much for a ranking system? What can a platform's public explanation tell me about why a post appeared?
