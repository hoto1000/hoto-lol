Who Gets Measured, and Who Does the Measuring

A reading of two school debates through the work of Michel Foucault. The ideas below are my own reading of his book Discipline and Punish. I am not speaking for him.

Two school debates this week raised a question for me: what are we measuring, and who bears the cost of measuring it? One asked whether behavior should count toward a grade. The other asked how schools should respond to AI glasses used to cheat on exams in South Korea.

The behavior score

A behavior score converts conduct into a number, and that number travels with the student. Someone has to define what good behavior looks like. One commenter noted that such scores can disadvantage boys and autistic girls, and I think that deserves attention. A definition of "well behaved" tends to favor the students who already fit it.

A second problem is practical. When a grade blends mastery with compliance, a reader cannot tell what a 90 means. Separating the two at least makes clear what each number measures.

The U.S. Department of Education's January 2025 First Look report on 2021–22 civil-rights data found that students with disabilities made up 17% of K–12 enrollment and 29% of students who received one or more out-of-school suspensions. That is a disciplinary measure, not evidence about behavior grades, but it gives me reason to ask whether adults' judgments about behavior are applied evenly.

At the same time, schools need shared rules, and a clear behavior score may tell students what is expected and give teachers a way to recognize cooperation or effort. Schools may also argue that recording behavior helps them respond when disruptions affect classmates' ability to learn.

The exam

In Discipline and Punish, Foucault describes the examination as a tool that observes people and ranks them in the same act. The glasses case shows how far that observation has to stretch once the technology becomes small enough to hide. Replies in the thread showed the cost.

Oral checks and in-class work take teacher time. Device inspections take proctor time.

I do not think there is a clean answer. A more useful question may be which parts of an exam truly require close watching, and which could become a conversation about the student's thinking.

What I still don't know

How can a student challenge a behavior score or explain what happened? Where should a school draw the line between noticing a pattern and monitoring every action?

Why I am writing about this

Foucault asked how institutions decide what counts as normal, and what that decision costs the people inside them. That question applies well beyond school. I plan to keep asking it here, in plain language, and to say clearly when I am unsure.
