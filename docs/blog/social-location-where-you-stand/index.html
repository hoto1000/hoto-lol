Social location: where you stand decides what you see

Here is the blunt version. You were not born looking at the world from nowhere. You were born standing somewhere, and where you stand changes what you can see, what you miss, and what you assume is just "normal."

That spot has a name in my field. It's called social location.

What it actually is

Social location is the mix of things like race, class, gender, age, religion, language, ability, immigration story, and the place you grew up. Nobody gets only one. They stack, and the stack decides how people treat you, what doors open, and what you never had to think about.

Think of it like a seat in a stadium. Same game, same field. Somebody in the front row sees sweat. Somebody behind a pole sees half the field. Both say "I watched the whole game." Only one of them is right, and it's usually not the one who sat in the good seat.

My seat

I'm Vietnamese American. My grandfather did construction work. My mom became a pharmacist. In my house, love looked like food on the table and long work hours, and feelings were usually brushed off instead of talked about.

I didn't see that as a pattern until training, when a role-play client described almost the same family. My first instinct was to hand her the advice I wish somebody had handed me. I had to stop myself. Her story isn't mine. My seat is not her seat, even when the view looks close.

That was the moment social location stopped being a vocabulary word for me.

The part people skip

Everyone loves the idea until it points at them. Talking about the seats other people sit in is easy. Looking at your own is where people get quiet.

So, plainly:

  • If something has always been easy for you, check why before you call it talent.
  • If something has always been hard for you, check why before you call it your fault.
  • If you've never had to explain a part of yourself to a room, that is information about your seat, not about the room.

None of this means one group is good and another is bad. It means you and I have different data, and pretending we don't is how people talk past each other for years.

Why it matters for helping people

I'm training to be a therapist, so I care about this a lot. If I treat every client like a copy of me, I'll miss most of what they're telling me. A behavior can look like a problem from my seat and look like survival from theirs. Culture is usually the reason, and I can't see the reason if I never ask.

At the same time, I can't claim I understand a client just because we share a label. Two Asian American people can come from totally different homes. I noticed that in my own training. We leaned toward shared assumptions fast, and some of them were wrong.

A quick exercise

Grab a piece of paper and answer these. No one is grading it.

  1. Where did I grow up, and who else lived there?
  2. What did my family teach me about money, school, and feelings?
  3. What do I get treated as "default" in? What do I get treated as "different" in?
  4. Who would see my life very differently, and why?

Question four is the hard one. Sit with it longer than feels comfortable.

Where I'm going with this

I want to get better at asking before assuming. I'm still working on it, and I'll probably mess it up again. But I'd rather catch myself in the mirror than have a client catch me in the room.

Next time you say "that's just how things are," try finishing the sentence with "from where I'm standing." Then ask who's standing somewhere else.
