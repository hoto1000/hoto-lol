Is the pressure to be happy making us less happy?

A Rest From Happiness

Oct 23, 2026 · 5 min read

Ideas from Arlie Hochschild

Happiness is lovely. Having to be happy is a job, and nobody told you about the shifts. This is a post about clocking out for a while.

I want to be clear before anyone worries: I am not against joy. I am against the rota. Somewhere in the last few decades, being happy stopped being something that happens to you on a good day and became something you are supposed to produce, on schedule, and display. The smile at work. The upbeat reply to a message you did not have the energy to read. The gratitude list at bedtime, written by someone who would rather just go to sleep. Each one is small. Together they are a second job, and the pay is terrible.

The job nobody applied for

The sociologist Arlie Hochschild had a name for this back in 1983: emotional labor. She was writing about flight attendants who were paid to look delighted for eight hours, but the idea escaped the aeroplane a long time ago. Most of us now do a version of it for free. We do it for bosses, for group chats, for relatives who ask how we are and visibly brace for the answer.

The researchers who study this draw a line between feeling an emotion and performing one. Performing an emotion you do not feel is called surface acting, and the evidence on it is fairly consistent: people who do a lot of it report more emotional exhaustion and more burnout than people who do less. That is not a surprise to anyone who has spent an evening being fun for other people and come home hollow. Pretending costs something. The bill comes later, when nobody is watching.

Why chasing it backfires

Here is the part that surprised me. A group of psychologists asked people how much they valued being happy, then measured how happy they actually were. The people who valued happiness most reported less of it, and the effect was strongest when life was going fine. The researchers' explanation is that holding a high standard for your own mood turns every ordinary day into a small failure. You were supposed to feel great. You feel okay. Now you feel okay and disappointed about it.

The same lab later followed people over time and found something close to the opposite: those who let their difficult feelings be there, without judging themselves for having them, had better mental health months later than people who fought every low mood as it arrived. And decades of work on emotional suppression, the habit of pushing feelings down so they do not show, links it to worse mood, not better, and to feeling less close to the people around you.

I should say what these studies are and are not. Most of them are correlational, which means they show that the pressure to be happy and feeling worse travel together, not which one drives the other. They are also mostly from one corner of the world. But they point the same way, and they match what I see: the people most frightened of a bad day are rarely the people having the fewest of them.

What a rest actually looks like

I am not proposing that you take up misery as a hobby. I am proposing something far more boring: that you stop doing the performance in a few places and see what happens.

  • Let a day be a six out of ten. Not a crisis, not a triumph. Just a Tuesday. Do not go looking for the reason it was not a nine.
  • Answer honestly, once. When someone you trust asks how you are, try "tired, honestly" instead of "good!" You will be amazed how often they say "me too."
  • Do not fix a feeling the moment it shows up. Let it sit on the sofa for an hour. Most of them leave on their own if you stop offering them tea.
  • Skip the gratitude list tonight. If it helps you, keep it. If it has become one more thing you are failing at, that is not gratitude, that is homework.
  • Tell one person you are off duty this week. "I am not up to being fun right now, but I would like to see you." The friends worth keeping will take that deal every time.
Happiness is a feeling. It is not a shift you have to cover.

Where I might be wrong

Positive emotions do real work. There is good research showing that moments of genuine joy and interest widen your attention and help you build friendships and skills you later lean on. I am not arguing against having them. I am arguing against being on the hook for them.

There is also a line I do not want anyone to miss. A rest from performing happiness is not the same as a mood that has gone flat and stayed flat. If you have felt low or empty most days for a couple of weeks, if sleep and appetite have changed, if nothing you used to like does anything for you anymore, that is not a rest. That is worth talking to someone about, and the practical side of this site exists for exactly that: how to find care, how to pay for it, and what to say on the first call. The crisis strip at the top of this page is there for the worst nights.

And some people are simply cheerful. I have met them. This post is not aimed at them, and they are welcome to carry on.

One step

This week, pick one place where you have been performing happy, and stop performing there. Not everywhere. Not forever. One group chat, one colleague, one evening. Notice whether the world ends. My bet is that it does not, and that you will feel a little less tired on Friday than you did last Friday.

You are allowed to be okay. Okay is a complete sentence. Give yourself a rest.

Where the facts come from

Sources

  • Arlie Russell Hochschild, The Managed Heart: Commercialization of Human Feeling (University of California Press, 1983). Coined "emotional labor" and the distinction between surface acting and deep acting.
  • Hülsheger & Schewe (2011), Journal of Occupational Health Psychology. A meta-analysis of emotional labor studies: surface acting is linked with emotional exhaustion and burnout.
  • Mauss, Tamir, Anderson & Savino (2011), Emotion. "Can seeking happiness make people unhappy?" People who valued happiness more reported lower well-being, most clearly when life stress was low. Mostly correlational, with one small experiment.
  • Ford, Lam, John & Mauss (2018), Journal of Personality and Social Psychology. Accepting negative emotions and thoughts, rather than judging them, predicted better psychological health over time.
  • Gross & John (2003), Journal of Personality and Social Psychology. Habitual emotional suppression is associated with worse mood and less social closeness.
  • Fredrickson (2001), American Psychologist. The counterpoint: the broaden-and-build theory of why positive emotions matter.
