A Rest From Happiness

Is the pressure to be happy making us less happy?

Oct 23, 2026 · 5 min read

Ideas from Arlie Hochschild

Forced happiness is a second job with terrible benefits. Joy is good. Being expected to perform it all day is a bad deal. I want to think about what clocking out looks like.

I support joy. The problem is the schedule. Over the last few decades, happiness seems to have become something people are expected to produce and display, even though it can also happen on a good day. Think of the smile at work, the upbeat reply to a message I do not have energy to read, and the gratitude list at bedtime when I would rather sleep. Each one can seem small. Together, they can feel like a second job, and I do not get much back for the work.

The job nobody applied for

Arlie Hochschild, a sociologist, gave this a name in 1983: emotional labor. She was writing about flight attendants who were paid to look delighted for eight hours. That idea reaches beyond airplanes. Many of us now do a version of it for free, at work, in group chats, and with relatives who ask how we are while visibly bracing for the answer.

Researchers who study this make a distinction between feeling an emotion and performing one. Performing an emotion you do not feel is called surface acting. The evidence on it is fairly consistent: people who do more of it report more emotional exhaustion and burnout than people who do less. That result tracks. An evening spent being fun for other people can leave someone feeling hollow when they get home. Pretending has a cost, and it may show up later, when nobody is watching.

Why chasing it backfires

One finding surprised me. A group of psychologists asked people how much they valued being happy, then measured how happy they actually were. People who valued happiness most reported less of it, and that effect was strongest when life was going fine. The researchers suggested that a high standard for your own mood can make ordinary days feel like small failures. You were supposed to feel great. You feel okay. Now you feel okay and disappointed about it. That is a lousy mood tax.

The same lab followed people over time and found a similar pattern in reverse: those who let difficult feelings be there without judging themselves for having them had better mental health months later than people who fought every low mood as it arrived. Decades of work on emotional suppression, the habit of pushing feelings down so they do not show, also links that habit with worse mood and less closeness with the people around you.

The limits matter. Most are correlational, which means they show that the pressure to be happy and feeling worse travel together, while leaving open which one drives the other. They also come mostly from one corner of the world. I still see them pointing the same way, and that fits what I notice: people most frightened of a bad day are rarely the people having the fewest of them.

What a rest actually looks like

My proposal is small: take a break from performing in a few places and pay attention to what happens.

  • Let a day be a six out of ten. A day can be a regular Tuesday, without a crisis or a triumph. I could leave it at six without searching for reasons it should have been a nine.
  • Answer honestly, once. When someone you trust asks how you are, I could answer "tired, honestly" in place of "good!" They might say "me too," though I cannot know how they will respond.
  • Do not fix a feeling the moment it shows up. I could give a feeling some time before trying to fix it. I could imagine letting it sit on the sofa for an hour, without offering it tea, and notice what happens when I do not rush to change it.
  • Skip the gratitude list tonight. If it helps, I can keep it another time. If it has become one more thing I feel I am failing at, it may feel more like homework.
  • Tell one person you are off duty this week. I could say, "I am not up to being fun right now, but I would like to see you." I cannot know how they will respond, but I can be honest about how much company I want.

Where I might be wrong

Positive emotions can do useful work. Research has found that moments of genuine joy and interest can widen your attention and help you build friendships and skills you may lean on later. I am glad people have those experiences. What concerns me is the pressure to be on the hook for them.

There is also a distinction I do not want anyone to miss. A break from performing happiness is different from a mood that has gone flat and stayed flat. If you have felt low or empty most days for a couple of weeks, if sleep and appetite have changed, or if nothing you used to like does anything for you anymore, it is worth talking to someone about it. The practical side of this site is there for exactly that: how to find care, how to pay for it, and what to say on the first call. The crisis strip at the top of this page is there for the worst nights.

Some people are simply cheerful. I have met them. This post is not aimed at them, and they are welcome to carry on.

One step

This week, I want to ease off the performance in one place where I have been doing it. One group chat, one colleague, or one evening is enough. I can notice what happens without turning it into a test of whether the world ends. I do not expect it to, and I want to see whether Friday feels any different from last Friday.

Where the facts come from

Sources

  • Arlie Russell Hochschild, The Managed Heart: Commercialization of Human Feeling (University of California Press, 1983). Coined "emotional labor" and the distinction between surface acting and deep acting.
  • Hülsheger & Schewe (2011), Journal of Occupational Health Psychology. A meta-analysis of emotional labor studies: surface acting is linked with emotional exhaustion and burnout.
  • Mauss, Tamir, Anderson & Savino (2011), Emotion. "Can seeking happiness make people unhappy?" People who valued happiness more reported lower well-being, most clearly when life stress was low. Mostly correlational, with one small experiment.
  • Ford, Lam, John & Mauss (2018), Journal of Personality and Social Psychology. Accepting negative emotions and thoughts, rather than judging them, predicted better psychological health over time.
  • Gross & John (2003), Journal of Personality and Social Psychology. Habitual emotional suppression is associated with worse mood and less social closeness.
  • Fredrickson (2001), American Psychologist. The counterpoint: the broaden-and-build theory of why positive emotions matter.
