The policy nobody voted on

Verdict: "I'm fine" is the most successful policy most families ever passed, and nobody voted on it.

Think about how well it works. Zero meetings. Zero paperwork. Everyone enforces it for free. Kids learn it by age six.

It also has a terrible track record.

How the policy gets passed

Nobody announces it. There's no family meeting where someone says, "From now on, feelings go in the drawer."

It passes the quiet way. A kid cries and gets told to stop. A kid gets angry and the room goes silent. A kid says "I'm fine" once, and everyone relaxes. That relief is the vote. The kid learns what keeps the peace.

In my house, feelings were usually brushed off instead of talked about. My dad was quiet and disciplined. Nobody was being cruel. They were running the policy they were handed.

Why it was a good idea once

Here is the part people skip when they roast this stuff. The policy usually had a reason.

When a family is rebuilding a life in a new country or a new economy, there's a lot of work and not much room. Someone has to show up to the job. Someone has to get the kids through school. "Keep it together" kept the lights on.

My grandfather did construction work. I doubt anyone on a job site had time to process feelings out loud. I'm not here to mock that. It got people through.

The policy made sense. The trouble is that it never got reviewed.

Where it breaks

A policy built for survival is a bad fit for everything after survival. Same rule, different situation, worse results.

Here's what "I'm fine" does once the emergency is over:

  • It makes small problems invisible until they're big ones.
  • It teaches people that needing help is a bad look.
  • It gives families a way to love each other without ever knowing each other.
  • It makes therapy sound like a scandal instead of a service.

That last one matters to me. I'm training to be a therapist. I've seen how fast "I'm fine" can end a conversation, and how many people think it's the only acceptable answer.

What I got wrong

I used to treat this as a flaw in other people's families. Then a role-play client described a household almost like mine, and my first move was to give her the advice I wish I'd gotten.

That was my own "I'm fine" in disguise. I wanted to fix it quickly so nobody had to sit in it. I had to stop and let her story be hers.

How to review a policy

You don't have to burn it down. You can just audit it.

  1. Name it. Say the rule out loud. "In my family, we say we're fine." Rules lose power when they're visible.
  2. Find the original reason. Ask who it protected and from what. Be fair about it.
  3. Check if the reason still applies. If the emergency is over, the policy might be out of date.
  4. Pick one small replacement. Not a big speech. Just a more honest answer once a week to someone safe.

Where I'm going with this

I want to get better at answering "how are you" with something other than "good." Small steps count. I'm still working on it.

And if you're the one who has always been "fine," you're not broken. You learned the policy well. You can read it again.
