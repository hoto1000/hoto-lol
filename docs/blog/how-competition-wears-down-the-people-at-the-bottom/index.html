How competition wears down the people at the bottom

If competition makes us better, why does it wear down the people at the bottom?

This is the script from my video series “Things therapy school taught me about society.” I wrote it with AI help from published sources, checked the facts, and recorded it in my own voice. What I said on camera may differ a little.

The race we’re told to run

We’re told competition makes everyone better. So why does it keep wearing down the people at the bottom?

I’m a therapy grad student, and I’m still learning. So take this as me thinking out loud.

In 1864, a philosopher named Herbert Spencer coined the phrase “survival of the fittest.” People took it and said the rich deserve to win and the poor deserve to lose. That idea stuck. Companies compete for profit, workers compete for jobs, and kids compete for spots in good schools.

I want to be fair. Competition can push prices down. Adam Smith wrote about that in 1776. Seventeen years earlier, he wrote a whole book on sympathy, which people tend to forget.

Who starts ahead

The problem is who starts the race ahead. In 1968, the sociologist Robert Merton named the Matthew effect, after a line in the Gospel of Matthew. He saw that famous scientists got more credit than unknown ones for similar work.

People have since seen the same pattern everywhere. The people who have more get more, and a head start turns into a bigger head start.

What losing costs

Losing the race costs your body and your mind. Starting in 1967, the Whitehall study followed about 17,000 British civil servants. The lower the job rank, the higher the death rate.

The men at the bottom died of heart disease at more than three times the rate of the men at the top, even after counting smoking, blood pressure, and cholesterol. In 2013, researchers found that when people with low incomes thought about money problems, their thinking dropped about as much as losing a full night of sleep.

In therapy school, we learn to look at the stress around a person before we blame the person. Being at the bottom of a race you didn’t choose is stressful.

Working together

That makes me angry. But I have hope. In 1902, Peter Kropotkin wrote that mutual aid is “as much a law of nature as mutual struggle.”

He saw animals and people survive by working together. You can see it today in credit unions, co-ops, tenant unions, and neighbors sharing rides and childcare.

Getting on the same team

Here are three things I’m trying.

One. Notice when I’m treating someone like competition, and ask if we could be on the same team.

Two. Share what I know, like a job lead or a study guide, with someone who didn’t get the head start.

Three. Move some of my money somewhere cooperative, like a credit union or a local co-op.

Where in your life do you feel like you’re always competing?
