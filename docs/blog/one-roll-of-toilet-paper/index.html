One roll of toilet paper: what a week of scarcity taught me

For one class assignment, I had to make a single roll of toilet paper last a whole week. That's it. No running to the store, no asking a roommate. One roll.

I thought it would be the easiest assignment of the semester. It turned out to be a small, annoying lesson in what scarcity does to your head.

Why it was easy for me, and why that's the point

I live in an apartment with my older brother, and he has his own bathroom. I didn't have to share, so I controlled how much I used. I also shop at Costco, so I can buy in bulk and stretch my money. One roll got me through the week without much trouble.

That already told me something. What was a mild inconvenience for me could be a real stressor for someone else.

What happened anyway

As the week went on, I started paying attention to the roll. I folded the sheets more carefully. I checked how much was left. I did the math on how long it would last.

Something I never think about turned into a small source of anxiety. I knew I could buy more the second the assignment ended, and it still took up more room in my head than I expected.

The part that felt like shame

The article we read, by Laszloffy and Habekost (2010), says this kind of task is meant to simulate daily life for people living in poverty. Scarcity isn't only having less. It brings stress, shame, and a constant need to keep track of what you have.

I never ran out. But I caught myself imagining what it would feel like to ask a classmate for a roll, and I felt embarrassed just thinking about it. That showed me how vulnerable it is to admit you don't have enough, even over something this small.

I used to think I was just frugal

I've always thought of myself as frugal. After this week I think I need to be more honest about that. For me, being frugal is a choice. I can plan ahead, buy in bulk, and save because I have the money to do it.

For a lot of people, frugal isn't optional. Money problems pile onto everything else and drain energy that people with more cushion don't even notice they have.

Why it matters for the work I want to do

I'm training to be a therapist. If a roll of toilet paper made me uncomfortable for a week, I can only imagine how much harder it is for someone to admit they can't provide food or shelter for their family.

That tells me clients need a place where they can say those things without feeling judged. A big part of my job will be to treat their courage and their resourcefulness with respect. Scarcity can make people creative, and it can also make them anxious, irritable, or ashamed, and I shouldn't assume which one I'm looking at.

Where I'm going with this

I want to keep checking my own privilege, because it's easy to forget when things are comfortable. Next time I hear someone called "bad with money," I want to ask what they were working with first.

Source: Laszloffy, T. A., & Habekost, J. (2010). Using experiential tasks to enhance cultural sensitivity among MFT trainees. Journal of Marital and Family Therapy, 36(3), 333-346. https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1752-0606.2010.00217.x
