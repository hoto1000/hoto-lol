What the Black Panthers and Dr. King both saw about poverty

What did the Black Panthers and Dr. King have in common?

A look at the Black Panther Party and Dr. Martin Luther King Jr. side by side. They disagreed about a lot, and putting them in one story is my own reading. I’m not speaking for either of them.

Two stories we usually keep apart

Most of us get taught these as two opposite stories. Dr. Martin Luther King Jr. is the peaceful dreamer. The Black Panther Party is the angry young men with guns.

The more I read, the more I think that split hides what they had in common. I’m still learning, and I could be wrong about parts of this.

They really did disagree. King believed in nonviolence his whole life. The Panthers, started by Huey P. Newton and Bobby Seale in Oakland in October 1966, believed in armed self-defense. I don’t want to blur that.

But by the late 1960s, both had landed on the same problem. It was poverty, and who the country was willing to leave hungry.

Breakfast as a revolution

In January 1969, the Panthers started serving free breakfast to kids at St. Augustine’s Episcopal Church in West Oakland. The idea was simple. Kids can’t learn when they’re hungry.

The program spread to Panther chapters in other cities that same year. The Party added free health clinics and testing for sickle cell anemia, a disease that hit Black families hard and got little attention from doctors. The sociologist Alondra Nelson wrote a whole book about this work, called Body and Soul.

The Panthers called these survival programs. What feels revolutionary to me is who did the work. Neighbors fed neighbors, and every bowl of cereal showed what the government wasn’t doing.

King’s turn toward the economy

King’s last years usually get skipped. In his 1967 book Where Do We Go from Here: Chaos or Community?, he wrote that “the solution to poverty is to abolish it directly by a now widely discussed measure: the guaranteed income.”

That April, at Riverside Church in New York, he named what he called the “giant triplets of racism, extreme materialism, and militarism.” Then he began planning the Poor People’s Campaign. The plan was to bring poor people of every race to Washington to demand jobs and an income.

In the spring of 1968, he went to Memphis to stand with Black sanitation workers on strike. They carried signs that said “I AM A MAN.” King was killed there on April 4, 1968.

The campaign went on without him. That May, thousands of people camped on the National Mall in a tent city they called Resurrection City.

The FBI saw it before most people did

This is the part that makes me angry. Under J. Edgar Hoover, the FBI ran a secret program called COINTELPRO. The FBI wiretapped King, and in 1964 it mailed him an anonymous letter that his staff read as urging him to kill himself.

The FBI also wrote that it wanted to stop the rise of a “messiah” who could unify the Black nationalist movement. It named King as a possible one if he ever gave up nonviolence.

In September 1968, Hoover called the Black Panther Party “the greatest threat to the internal security of the country.” The next July, FBI headquarters wrote to its San Francisco office that the breakfast program was “a potentially successful effort by the BPP to teach children to hate police.” The FBI was scared of free breakfast.

In December 1969, police raided the Chicago apartment of Fred Hampton, the 21-year-old leader of the Illinois Panthers, and killed him in his bed. An FBI informant had given them a floor plan of the apartment.

Hampton had built what he called a Rainbow Coalition with the Young Lords, a Puerto Rican group, and the Young Patriots, poor white Southerners living in Chicago. Poor people of every color working together was the same thing King was trying to build with the Poor People’s Campaign. I think that’s why both scared the people in power.

How we found out

In March 1971, a small group of people broke into an FBI office in Media, Pennsylvania, just outside Philadelphia. They mailed the files to reporters. One file had a word most of the public had never seen: COINTELPRO.

In 1975 and 1976, a Senate committee led by Frank Church laid out what the FBI had done. It’s hard for me to read. It also gives me some hope, because ordinary people near my hometown are the reason we know.

Why I think it was one of the greatest revolutions

Neither one won everything it fought for. Poverty is still here, and I don’t want to pretend otherwise.

But I think they changed what a lot of people believe they’re owed. The government started the School Breakfast Program as a pilot in 1966 and made it permanent in 1975. I can’t say the Panthers caused that, and I don’t want to claim more than the history shows. I do think they made hungry kids much harder to ignore.

To me, a revolution is a change in what ordinary people expect from each other and from their country. King gave a lot of people the words for that. The Panthers showed what it looked like on a Tuesday morning before school.

What I’m trying

Both of them started with a need right in front of them. Here’s what I’m trying, and you’re welcome to try it with me.

One. I’m reading this history with friends in a reading circle. The Young Lords are on our list.

Two. I’m looking for one need in my own city that neighbors could meet together. If you’re in Philly, I keep a list of places to start.

Three. I’m mapping my own social location, so I can see which needs I never had to notice.

I want to keep learning from both of them, and practice noticing who’s hungry near me before I decide who’s to blame.
