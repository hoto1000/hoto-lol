Why does help so often fail to reach men?

What we teach men about pain: stigma, silence, and the help that misses them

Oct 27, 2026 · 5 min read

A note before we start: I am doing alright. I am writing this to put some light on a situation I see around me, and I am not writing it because I am in crisis.

I am training to become a marriage and family therapist, and one number keeps following me around. According to the CDC, males make up about half of the U.S. population but nearly 80% of suicide deaths (2023 figures), and in 2024 the male suicide rate was nearly four times the female rate. I read that and started thinking about the men in my own life.

As an Asian American man, I grew up around the idea that hard feelings were something you handled quietly and on your own. Nobody had to say it out loud. I do not think the people around me were wrong for living that way, because they were doing what they had been taught too. But I have started to wonder what that silence costs, and why help so often fails to reach men. This post is my attempt to look at that question with curiosity, and without blaming men or women.

What the numbers show

Suicide is one piece of a larger picture, and I want to keep the numbers calm and in proportion. The CDC reports the sex disparity year after year, and a federal report covering 2014 to 2024 shows the male rate running roughly three to four times the female rate across that decade.

Treatment looks different. In NIMH's summary of 2022 survey data, 41.6% of men with any mental illness got treatment in the past year, compared with 56.9% of women. Among adults with serious mental illness, the figures were 59.3% for men and 71.4% for women.

Women are diagnosed with depression and anxiety at higher rates, so a higher treatment rate for women does not mean women are doing fine. It does suggest that for men who are struggling, care is reaching fewer of them. I am careful here, because the survey counts only people who answered in English and only the categories male and female, so it cannot tell the whole story.

Is distress being missed in men?

Part of the gap might be what we look for. A 2013 study in JAMA Psychiatry by Martin and colleagues found that men reported more anger attacks, substance use, and risk-taking when they were depressed, symptoms that standard depression checklists do not ask about. When those were added to the usual symptoms, the difference in depression rates between men and women disappeared in their sample.

That is one study, and I would say distress may be missed in some men and stop short of saying it is missed. Still, it made me think about how a man could be struggling and have it show up as irritability or drinking, and how a screening form might never ask about either one.

The rules many of us inherit

The American Psychological Association's guidelines for working with boys and men say that some traits linked with masculinity, like courage and leadership, are often tied to good health, while other norms can do harm. Men who are taught to bury their feelings tend to be less willing to seek help.

A meta-analysis by Wong and colleagues (2017) looked at 78 samples with more than 19,000 participants. They found that conforming to masculine norms was modestly linked with worse mental health and moderately linked with less help-seeking. Self-reliance was among the norms most consistently tied to poor outcomes. Primacy of work was not significantly related to any outcome. These are correlations, so the findings say these things go together and do not show that one causes the other.

I find that last detail interesting. It suggests some of what we call masculine values, like responsibility and providing for others, may be fine, and that the pressure to handle everything alone is the part more closely tied to trouble. I could be reading too much into one analysis, so I hold it loosely.

Where my own story fits, and where it does not

I can only speak for myself. Research on Asian Americans and mental health services finds that Asian Americans use services at lower rates than the general population, and that U.S.-born Asian Americans use them more than immigrants do (Abe-Kim et al., 2007). Family expectations, immigration, and generation all seem to matter, and Asian American men are a very large and varied group, so my experience is one among many.

What I notice, from a family systems view, is that nobody in a family has to be the villain for a rule like “handle it quietly” to get passed down. Each person is usually doing what made sense to them in their situation.

What might help

I do not have a fix, and I am wary of anyone who says they do. A few ideas seem worth sitting with. Help might land better when it is described in terms men already value, like getting stronger, solving a problem, or looking after the people who depend on them. Families, friends, and workplaces can make it normal to ask a second question after “how are you.” And clinicians can learn to ask about anger, sleep, drinking, and risk-taking, along with sadness.

I wonder how many men would talk to someone if the first conversation felt like a practical step, and I imagine the answer depends a lot on the person.

If you or someone you know is struggling

In the U.S., you can call or text 988 to reach the Suicide and Crisis Lifeline at any time. If someone is in immediate danger, call 911. This post is a personal reflection and does not offer medical advice or treatment.
