Where the wages went

If workers make so much more per hour than they did 50 years ago, why doesn't a typical paycheck show it?

In the first entry of this series I tried to say plainly what I mean by capitalism. This one is about a single question that kept coming up when I read about it. If American workers produce so much more every hour than they used to, where did the extra money go?

The gap in one picture

Economists measure productivity as how much the economy turns out for each hour people work. When productivity rises, there is more value to split, so you would expect pay to rise with it. For a long time it did.

The Economic Policy Institute (EPI), a labor-leaning think tank, tracks this. It says pay for typical workers “climbed together with productivity from 1948 until the late 1970s.” Then the lines split. Using EPI’s own index, from 1979 to 2022 net productivity grew about 65 percent while the pay of typical workers grew about 15 percent, after inflation. EPI’s headline puts it this way: “Productivity has grown 4.4x as much as pay.” (EPI last updated that page in October 2022, so those are the newest numbers it shows.)

“Typical worker” here means production and nonsupervisory workers, the people who aren’t bosses. EPI says they make up roughly 80 percent of the workforce. Pay here counts benefits as well as wages.

The Bureau of Labor Statistics (BLS) shows the same split in its own chart of productivity and real hourly pay since 1973, which it labels “commonly referred to as the wage gap.” BLS figures also show that productivity growth itself slowed after 1973, from about 3.2 percent a year in 1947 to 1973 to about 1.3 percent a year in 1973 to 1979. So part of the story is slower growth for everyone. The other part is who got the growth there was.

Where the difference went

Money doesn’t vanish. If typical workers didn’t get it, someone else did. Here are the main places it went, using the most recent numbers I could find.

Top earners. EPI’s analysis of Social Security wage records found that from 1979 to 2023, wages for the top 1 percent grew about 182 percent. Wages for the top 0.1 percent grew about 354 percent. Wages for the bottom 90 percent grew about 44 percent.

CEOs. EPI’s latest CEO pay study found that in 2024, CEOs at big companies were paid 281 times what a typical worker made. In 1965 that ratio was about 21 to 1. Realized CEO pay rose 1,094 percent from 1978 to 2024. Typical worker pay rose 26 percent over the same years.

Owners and profits. BLS measures the labor share, the part of business income that goes to workers as pay. In the nonfarm business sector it fell by about 2 percentage points from the late 1940s to around 2000, then fell faster. It dropped below 60 percent for the first time in 2005 and hit a low of 56.0 percent in late 2011. A smaller labor share means a bigger share going to the people who own the business.

The honest argument

I want to be fair here, because not everyone reads these numbers the same way.

Michael Strain, an economist at the conservative American Enterprise Institute, argues that the link between pay and productivity is stronger than the EPI chart suggests. He points to work by Robert Lawrence showing that if you count total compensation (wages plus benefits) for the average worker, and adjust for prices the way producers see them, productivity and pay were “coincident between 1970 and 2001.” After that Lawrence’s chart shows a divergence “but not a dramatic one.” Strain’s own view is that “wages and productivity are strongly linked.”

A BLS study from 2017 helps explain why the two sides get different answers. One reason is inflation math. Pay is usually adjusted with consumer prices, the things workers buy. Output is adjusted with the prices of what workers make. Those prices rose at different speeds, especially in tech, where computers kept getting cheaper. The BLS authors said this difference “contributed to the overall gap.” They also found that “the labor share of income declined in 77 percent of industries studied.” So the measurement choice explains part of the gap. A real shift away from workers explains part of it too.

Here is how I hold all this. The critics mostly compare productivity to the average worker. The average gets pulled up by people at the top. EPI compares it to the typical worker. Both can be true at once: total pay kept up better than the EPI chart shows, and much of that pay went to the top. That second part is the story.

Why it happened

EPI argues that the split was a set of choices. Starting in the late 1970s, it says, “Raises in the federal minimum wage became smaller and rarer,” “Labor law failed to keep pace with growing employer hostility toward unions,” and “Tax rates on top incomes were lowered.”

Unions are the clearest piece I can measure. In 1983, the first year of the BLS series, 20.1 percent of wage and salary workers were union members. In 2025 it was 10.0 percent. Unions matter for pay. In 2025, full-time union members had median weekly earnings of $1,404, compared with $1,174 for nonunion workers. Other things differ between those groups too, so the whole gap isn’t the union itself. Still, fewer unions means fewer people at the table when the gains get split.

I get why this happened. Companies answer to shareholders, and every dollar that doesn’t go to wages can go to them. Nobody had to be a cartoon villain. The system rewarded the people who already had the most, and it kept doing that for decades.

What workers have done about it

Workers haven’t sat still. Union membership grew by about 410,000 people in 2025, to 14.7 million, even as the rate stayed about even. BLS counted 30 major strikes and lockouts that began in 2025, involving 306,800 workers. That’s above the average of about 21 a year from 2016 to 2025, though far below the peak of 470 in 1952.

Where I want to go next

I still want an economy that grows. I want the people doing the work to get a fair share of what they make. If you’re not sure where to start, look up whether your workplace or industry has a union, read your state’s minimum wage law, or ask a coworker what they think fair pay would be.
