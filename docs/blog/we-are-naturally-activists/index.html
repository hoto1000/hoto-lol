We are naturally activists

If we all see what's wrong, why does almost nobody get up?

An opinion piece. The history and research are sourced below. The only personal lines are my own words from an interview, and the argument is mine.

I. The intercom

In 1968, a college student in New York sat alone in a small booth with a pair of headphones. Through the intercom came the voice of another student, talking about life in the city. Then the voice began to stutter. It said it was having a seizure. It asked for help. Then it went quiet.

The voice was a recording. The study was run by two psychologists, John Darley and Bibb Latané, and the question they were asking was simple. Who gets up?

When students believed they were the only one listening, 85 percent left the booth to find help before the recording ended. When they believed four other people were listening too, 31 percent did. The rest sat there. Most of them were not cold people. Many were sweating and shaking when the researchers came in. They cared. They just assumed someone else would go.

Darley and Latané called it diffusion of responsibility. I call it the story of my country.

II. Everyone can see it

We know more than any generation before us. We know what rent costs and what wages buy. We know how long the wait is for a therapist, and who can afford to skip the wait. We can explain all of it in a group chat at midnight, with links.

And then we put the phone down.

That makes me angry. It makes me angry at the system, and it makes me angry at us, and I count myself in “us.” We are millions of students in millions of booths, all hearing the same voice through the intercom. Everyone can see it. So everyone waits.

I understand why. People are tired. They work long hours and come home to bills. Tiredness is real, and it is also the most useful thing a broken system can produce in the people living under it. A tired person does not organize. A tired person scrolls.

III. Before anyone taught us otherwise

Here is where the anger turns into something else.

In 2006, two researchers in Germany, Felix Warneken and Michael Tomasello, invited toddlers about eighteen months old into a lab. An adult would drop a clothespin and reach for it, unable to get it. Or the adult would walk toward a cabinet with arms full of magazines and bump into the closed doors.

The toddlers got up. They waddled over, picked up the clothespin, and handed it back. They opened the cabinet doors. Nobody asked them. Nobody paid them. Most of them could barely say a full sentence, and they already knew what to do when someone needed a hand.

A century earlier, a Russian prince named Peter Kropotkin had spent years watching animals across the cold plains of Siberia. Everyone around him was reading Darwin as a story about competition, the strong beating the weak. Kropotkin saw something else in the snow. He saw animals huddling, warning each other, hunting together, surviving together. In 1902 he published Mutual Aid: A Factor of Evolution, arguing that cooperation shaped life as much as struggle did.

And more than two thousand years ago, Aristotle wrote that the human being is by nature a political animal. We live in groups. We decide things together. We are made for it.

So when I say we are naturally activists, I mean it as plainly as I can. The toddler who picks up the clothespin is us before the world teaches us to sit in the booth.

IV. The point is to change it

This is why I think accountability and action are among the most important traits a socialist can have. The whole idea is people sharing, deciding together, and looking after each other. You cannot hold that idea only in your head. An idea about sharing that never shares anything is just a nice thought.

In 1845, a young Karl Marx scribbled eleven short notes in a notebook. The last one became famous after his death: “The philosophers have only interpreted the world, in various ways; the point is to change it.”

More than a hundred years later, a Brazilian teacher named Paulo Freire was jailed after a military coup and forced into exile. In Chile he finished Pedagogy of the Oppressed. He wrote that real change needs praxis, “reflection and action upon the world in order to transform it.” Thinking without doing becomes talk. Doing without thinking becomes noise. A movement needs both, in the same body, every day.

When someone asked me about this before, I said it the only way I know how. “I believe the change is going to come if you put in the right efforts.”

The efforts are the part I want to be held to.

V. Breakfast at St. Augustine’s

In January 1969, the Black Panther Party started serving breakfast to children at St. Augustine’s Episcopal Church in West Oakland. The church’s rector, Father Earl Neil, opened the doors. Volunteers showed up before school with eggs and grits and toast. The kids ate. Then the volunteers cleaned up and came back the next morning, and the morning after that.

That is what accountability looks like up close. Kids were hungry. People said they would feed them. Then they kept their word, again and again, until it became a habit, and the habit spread to city after city.

Frederick Douglass stood in front of a crowd in Canandaigua, New York, in August 1857 and said, “Power concedes nothing without a demand. It never did and it never will.” A demand is a promise you make out loud. Accountability is what happens the next day, when you have to show up and keep it.

VI. Belonging to it

Grace Lee Boggs was born in 1915 to Chinese immigrant parents. She earned a doctorate in philosophy, could not get hired at a university, and moved to Detroit. She stayed there and organized for more than sixty years, until she died at one hundred.

In a 2003 interview at her home in Detroit, she said, “I’ve come to believe that you cannot change any society unless you take responsibility for it, unless you see yourself as belonging to it and responsible for changing it.”

Belonging is the word I keep coming back to. The students in the booths did not feel like the emergency belonged to them. The volunteers at St. Augustine’s did. The difference between those two rooms is the whole argument of this post.

VII. What I’m trying

I don’t have this figured out. I’m writing it partly to hold myself to it.

I want to pick one thing and do it on a schedule, small enough that I can’t talk myself out of it. I want to do it with other people, because a promise made in a group is harder to break. In my own words, it means “keeping in contact and in close communication with the ones organizing.”

I want to be honest when I drop the ball, and come back anyway. And I never want this to turn into “breaking people’s boundaries in conversation.” Be respectful about it.

If you want a place to start, my Philly page lists groups already doing the work, and the reading circle is a way to learn with friends and then do something together.

Somewhere right now, a voice is coming through the intercom. I want to be the one who gets up from the booth, and I want to find out what happens when enough of us do.
