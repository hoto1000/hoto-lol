The "I Am" poem and who we say we are

What do I choose to put after “I am”?

What it is

An “I Am” poem is a simple template. Every line starts with the same two words, “I am,” and you finish the sentence. Some versions also use “I wonder,” “I hear,” “I want,” and “I hope.” The structure is small, so there’s nowhere to hide.

I wrote mine for my capstone class, as part of my minor in Counseling and Social Change. We were asked to write it, share it with the class, and talk about it afterward. Writing it was easy. Reading it out loud in front of the whole class was the hard part.

Where people start

A lot of people start with identity, and a lot of those lines are about heritage. A few examples of what someone might write:

  • I am Vietnamese American.
  • I am Italian American.
  • I am Hispanic.
  • I am a mutt.

They look similar on paper, but each one carries a different story. “I am Vietnamese American” can hold a whole family history of migration. “I am a mutt” is usually said with a laugh, and it tells you the person has a mix of backgrounds and doesn’t have one tidy label for it.

In an August 12, 2021 report on the 2020 Census, the U.S. Census Bureau counted 33.8 million people who identified with two or more races. The Bureau also cautioned that comparisons with 2010 need care because the race questions, processing, and coding changed. Read the Census Bureau’s report and its caveat.

The part I noticed

Labels like these are the ones we’ve been handed by forms, census boxes, and the questions people ask when they meet us. Writing “I am” and finishing it yourself is different, because you choose which label goes first and whether it fits at all.

At the same time, a self-chosen line cannot control how other people or institutions label us. A short poem may be too small for an identity that keeps changing.

Culture matters here. My Vietnamese American background grounds me the most and gives me the most direction. Being Christian is part of my answer too, and that’s something I’m still grappling with. Some people lead with their heritage. Others skip it and write about what they do or who they love. Both are real answers.

Why I keep writing these

I took a class on finding your identity, and I write things like this because I hope my answers stay true over time and that the writing helps. Some of it is still unfinished, and I’m okay with that.

Try it yourself

Give yourself ten minutes and start every line with “I am.” Don’t edit while you write. Then read it back and see which line you almost left out.

You don’t have to share it. I shared mine in class, but I’m keeping it off the blog for now.

What I still don’t know

  • Will the lines I choose still feel true next year?
  • What might I leave out when I only have ten minutes?
  • How much do other people’s questions shape which line I put first?

Check it yourself

  • The Census Bureau’s report on how people reported race in 2020.
  • The Census Bureau’s 2020 Census data tables, including the redistricting supplementary tables.
  • Connecticut Department of Children and Families’ “Who I Am” poem exercise.

I’m a student sharing what I’m learning. This isn’t therapy or clinical advice.

Next, I want to write another one in a year and see which lines have changed.
