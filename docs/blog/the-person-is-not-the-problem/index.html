The person is not the problem: what narrative therapy taught me

What changes when we separate a person from their problem?

This is the script from my video series “Things therapy school taught me about society.” I wrote it with AI help from published sources, checked the facts, and recorded it in my own voice. What I said on camera may differ a little.

The problem is the problem

Something I learned in therapy school that changed how I see the world. Part two.

In narrative therapy, Michael White and David Epston taught a simple idea. The person is not the problem. The problem is the problem.

So a therapist might talk about “the worry” or “the anger” as something that shows up in a person’s life. That gives people room to step back from the problem and push against it together.

How we talk about homelessness

When I learned this, I thought about how we talk about people without housing. We say “the homeless,” like it’s who they are. Losing housing is something that happens to people. Rent goes up, a job ends, a hospital bill comes.

Changing how I talk

I’m still catching myself on this. Here are three things I’m trying.

One. I say “people experiencing homelessness,” or I use someone’s name if I know it.

Two. When I hear a story about someone on the street, I ask what happened before, like a rent increase or an eviction.

Three. I look up one local group that helps with housing and see what they need.

Check it yourself

  • White, M., & Epston, D. (1990). Narrative Means to Therapeutic Ends.
