How great thinkers are made

2026-10-23

Nobody becomes a great thinker by aiming at greatness. The people we remember, like Aristotle, Hobbes, Madison, Arendt and Rawls, got obsessed with one real problem. Then they worked on it longer and harder than anyone else. The greatness came after.

So the real goal is simple to say and hard to do. Find your problem. Then build habits that let you think about it better than anyone alive.

Here are six habits that build that kind of mind.

1. Read the originals, not summaries

Summaries tell you what a thinker concluded. The original shows you how they got there. That is the part you actually need.

A starting shelf for political thought:

  • Plato, Republic
  • Aristotle, Politics
  • Machiavelli, The Prince
  • Hobbes, Leviathan
  • Locke, Second Treatise of Government
  • Hamilton, Madison and Jay, The Federalist Papers
  • Tocqueville, Democracy in America
  • Mill, On Liberty
  • Marx and Engels, The Communist Manifesto
  • Arendt, The Human Condition
  • Rawls, A Theory of Justice

Read slowly. Argue in the margins. Ask one question of every author: what were they afraid of? Every political theory is an answer to a fear.

2. Write every day

A thought you never write down stays fuzzy. Writing forces you to find out what you really believe.

Try one short piece a day. Take a position and defend it. It does not have to be good. It has to exist.

3. Make the best case for the other side

Great thinkers can explain the opposing view better than its own fans can. If you cannot make the strongest case for a view you reject, you do not understand it yet.

Before you argue against something, write its best defense first.

4. Test ideas against real people

Ideas that never touch real life drift into fantasy. Politics is about people, so check your theories against how people actually behave. Watch how they fear, bond, fight and change.

I am training as a family therapist, and this is the habit I care about most. Sitting with real families teaches you things about power, trust and belonging that no book can.

5. Find people who will push back

Ideas get sharper in argument. A reading group, a mentor, or a friend who disagrees with you and says so will teach you more than any single book.

6. Give it decades

Most of the books on that shelf came after twenty years or more of this kind of work. There is no shortcut. That is not bad news. It means the people who keep going are the ones who get there.

Where to start this week

Pick one book from the list. Read ten pages a day. Write one paragraph about what you read, and one paragraph arguing with it. That is the whole method, repeated for a long time.
