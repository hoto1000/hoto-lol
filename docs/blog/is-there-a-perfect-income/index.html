Is there a perfect income? Why happiness seems to level off

Is there an amount of money after which more stops making us happier?

Oct 27, 2026 · 5 min read

Ideas from Daniel Kahneman, Angus Deaton, Matthew Killingsworth, Barbara Mellers, Andrew Jebb, Erzo Luttmer

For a long time I believed there was a number. If I could earn a certain amount, the stress would go away and I could finally relax. I think a lot of people my age carry some version of that number around in their heads.

Growing up Asian American, money in my family carried a lot of weight. My grandfather did construction work, and my mom became a pharmacist. Every step up felt like it was paid for by the generation before. So when I started reading the research on income and happiness, I wanted to know if that number was real.

Where the "$75,000" idea came from

In 2010, Daniel Kahneman and Angus Deaton looked at more than 450,000 answers to a daily Gallup survey of Americans. They split happiness into two parts. One was life evaluation, which is how you rate your life when you step back and think about it. The other was emotional well-being, which is how your days actually feel: how often you felt joy, stress, sadness, or anger.

Life evaluation kept rising as income went up. Emotional well-being rose too, but past about $75,000 a year it stopped improving in their data. That finding became a headline people still repeat: money buys happiness up to $75,000, and then it stops.

The number moved

In 2018, a team led by Andrew Jebb at Purdue used the Gallup World Poll, a sample of more than 1.7 million people. They found the leveling-off point worldwide at about $95,000 for life evaluation and $60,000 to $75,000 for emotional well-being. The researchers said those amounts are for one person and would likely be higher for families. The point came later in wealthier regions, and in some parts of the world, people who earned past it actually rated their lives a little lower.

That made me realize a "perfect income" depends a lot on where you live and what the people around you have.

Then someone found no plateau at all

In 2021, Matthew Killingsworth at Penn tried a different method. He collected about 1.7 million in-the-moment check-ins from 33,391 working adults in the US, asking how they felt right then. In his data, both kinds of happiness kept rising with income, and he found no plateau above $75,000.

So two careful studies disagreed. What I liked about what happened next is that the researchers worked it out together. Kahneman and Killingsworth teamed up with a third researcher, Barbara Mellers, to reanalyze the data.

Both were right, for different people

Their 2023 paper found that for most people, happiness keeps climbing as income rises. For the least happy 15 to 20 percent, happiness rose with income up to about $100,000 (the old threshold adjusted for inflation) and then mostly flattened. At the happiest end, it actually climbed faster above $100,000.

The way I understand it, money can fix a lot of the problems that make life miserable, like rent, debt, a car that keeps breaking down, or not being able to see a doctor. Kahneman and Deaton also found that low income makes things like divorce, illness, and being alone hurt more. Once those money problems are handled, what is left for that unhappy group is often stuff money can't reach, like grief, loneliness, or depression.

I want to be honest that this isn't settled. In 2024, other researchers questioned how strong the flattening really is and whether the data can show money causing the change. So I hold the $100,000 number loosely. The bigger pattern across all these studies still seems pretty consistent.

Why it levels off

Money works on a curve. In these studies, happiness tracks the percentage change in income better than the dollar change. Going from $30,000 to $60,000 doubles your income. Going from $200,000 to $230,000 is the same $30,000, but it's only a 15 percent raise, and it feels much smaller.

We get used to things. Psychologists call this hedonic adaptation. A raise or a nicer apartment feels great for a while, and then it becomes normal. Jebb's team said their results point to some of this adaptation, and to the way our wants grow as our income grows.

We compare ourselves to the people around us. An economist named Erzo Luttmer found that, at the same personal income, people whose neighbors earned more reported being less happy. That fits the finding that the leveling-off point is higher in wealthier regions. When everyone around you earns more, "enough" goes up too.

Some of what shapes a good day has little to do with money. In Kahneman and Deaton's data, health, caregiving, loneliness, and smoking predicted people's daily emotions more strongly than income and education did. Money can support those things, but it can't stand in for them.

What this means for me

Looking back, I think the number I carried around was mostly about safety. The research backs that part up. Getting out of real financial stress does change how life feels.

What I'm trying to unlearn is the idea that the number keeps going forever. As someone training to be a therapist, I want to remember that when people talk about money, they are often also talking about security, worth, or what their family expects of them. I'm still working on separating those for myself.

Over the next few years, I want to figure out what "enough" looks like for me, and then put more of my energy into time, health, and the people I care about.
