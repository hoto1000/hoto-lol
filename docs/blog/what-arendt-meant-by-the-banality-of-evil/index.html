How do ordinary people take part in terrible things without deciding anything?

What Arendt meant by the banality of evil

Oct 27, 2026 · 4 min read

Ideas from Hannah Arendt, Bettina Stangneth

In the spring of 1961, a mild-mannered man in a glass booth sat in a Jerusalem courtroom and answered questions about the deaths of millions of people. Adolf Eichmann had run the logistics of the Nazi deportations. He had been captured in Argentina by Israeli agents the year before, and he was now on trial for crimes against the Jewish people. The philosopher Hannah Arendt went to watch, and she wrote about it for The New Yorker. Two words from that coverage, “banality of evil,” have been quoted, misquoted, and argued over ever since.

What she saw in the courtroom

Many people expected a monster. What Arendt described was a man who could barely speak without reaching for stock phrases and clichés. The Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy summarizes her view this way: she saw a “clown” where many expected a “monster,” and she attributed his deeds in part to “thoughtlessness,” meaning an inability to look at anything from another person's point of view.

The book that followed, Eichmann in Jerusalem: A Report on the Banality of Evil, carried the phrase in its subtitle and used it once more near the end. Arendt later described the point in a letter to the scholar Gershom Scholem. Evil, she wrote, is never “radical.” It is only extreme, and it can lay waste to the world because it spreads “like a fungus on the surface.”

What the phrase does not say

The phrase is often taken to mean that evil is boring, or that Eichmann was a harmless clerk who followed orders. Arendt never wrote that. By her account he pursued the deportations with zeal, and she supported his death sentence. She believed he was fully responsible for what he did.

The phrase also describes one man's character and motives. It is a claim about how Eichmann appeared to her in court, and the Stanford Encyclopedia notes that it was never meant as a general theory of Nazi evil.

The argument that followed

The criticism started right away, and some of it has held up. Many critics, including the historian Bettina Stangneth in Eichmann Before Jerusalem, argue that Arendt took a performance at trial for the real man. On that reading, Eichmann was a convinced antisemite who presented himself in court as a bureaucrat. Her book also drew anger for its tone and for its claims about the Jewish Councils, which were bodies the Nazis forced Jewish communities to form.

I think both things can be true at once. A single trial may have shown Arendt a face that was partly staged. Her larger question still stands on its own: how do ordinary people take part in terrible things without ever feeling that they have decided anything?

Thinking as a habit

Arendt spent her later work on that question. She asked whether “the habit of examining whatever happens to come to pass” might be one of the conditions that keep people from doing evil. Her answer involved what she called thinking: the silent dialogue a person has with themselves.

In her essay on personal responsibility under dictatorship, she described the few people who refused to collaborate. They did not hold firmer moral rules than their neighbors. They asked themselves whether they could still live in peace with themselves after doing a certain deed. “I cannot walk away from myself,” she wrote.

That is a modest standard. It does not require heroism or a philosophy degree. It asks for a pause long enough to ask what an action is, and who it lands on.

Why it still gets quoted

Arendt was writing about totalitarian rule, and the comparison should be made carefully. Most workplace shortcuts and bureaucratic habits are nowhere near the Final Solution. Still, her observation carries over in a small way to ordinary life. People repeat the phrases of their institution, follow the form, and tell themselves that the decision belongs to someone else.

Noticing when that happens is a skill. It can be practiced with small things first: a policy you repeat without having read, a line you pass along because everyone else does. The point is to catch the moment when a phrase is doing the thinking for you.
