What should school feel like?

A school should be a harbor, not a sorting machine

Oct 24, 2026 · 4 min read

Most of what makes a school feel safe or unsafe is small. A snack you can take without being watched. A room you can sit in without being in trouble. A lunch line that does not announce who owes money. Nobody remembers the policy. They remember whether they felt wanted.

I am training as a family therapist, so I think about a school the way I think about a home. The rules matter less than the feeling underneath them. Here are eight ideas, each with a real change underneath, that a school could try without a big budget or a new law. None of them ask a student to explain or prove anything.

1. A school should be a harbor, not a sorting machine

A sorting machine ranks you on the way in. A harbor takes you as you arrive.

The change underneath: a calm welcome at the door, one adult every student knows by name and can go to, and a plain, shame-free way to catch up after missing class. Missing a day should cost you the lesson, not your standing.

2. Help without a spotlight

Some students need school supplies, hygiene items, or a snack, and they will go without before they ask in front of anyone.

The change: put those things somewhere a student can take them without asking, without a form, and without proving need. A shelf. A drawer. A corner of the nurse's office. Then ask students privately whether the setup feels private and dignified, and fix whatever they say is off.

This is the one I would start with. It is small, loving, and easy to test.

3. A soft landing before the first bell

Give students a few optional, ungraded minutes at the start of the day to settle, read, or sit near an adult they trust.

No forced check-in. No sharing circle. No questions about how they are doing unless they bring it up. The point is not to find out what is wrong. The point is to let the day start gently.

4. Quiet is part of belonging

Let students help design a calm, accessible space that anyone can use for a short while, no reason required.

Two rules keep it honest. It is never a punishment room. And it is never a stand-in for real help when someone needs more than quiet. If the same student is there every day, that is information for a caring adult, not a reason to close the door.

5. The rules should hear the people who live under them

Pick one everyday policy, like phones, hallway passes, or late work, and let students help shape it.

Say up front what can change and what cannot, and why. Then show them how their input changed the final decision, or did not, and be honest about which. Students can handle a no. What they cannot handle is being asked and then ignored.

6. Teach peace as a skill

Listening, repairing a conflict, and disagreeing without humiliating someone are skills. Nobody is born with them.

Give students optional practice: short, low stakes, and never used as discipline. The goal is not fewer fights on paper. The goal is students who know what to do after one.

7. The lunch table is part of the social contract

A kid should be able to eat without being shamed for a balance their family owes.

The change: make nourishing meals easy to get, end lunch debt shaming in every form, from stamps on hands to taking a tray away, and give students a real say in what is served. A school that feeds students without conditions is telling them something about their worth.

8. A library should feel like a second living room

Keep it open beyond class hours. Books, internet, quiet, and an adult who helps without asking why you need it.

For some students the library is the only calm, warm, free place with Wi-Fi in their whole week. Run it like that matters, because it does.

What these have in common

Every one of these protects two things: privacy and choice. Nobody has to explain themselves. Nobody has to join. Frame each one as a small experiment in dignity and belonging, try it for a semester, and ask students privately how it felt. Keep what they say works. Drop what they say does not.

I do not know how to make a whole school feel like a harbor. I am not sure anyone does. But a shelf is a start.

Where to start this week

Start with help without a spotlight. Pick one shelf. Stock it. Tell students it is there and that nobody will ask why they used it. A month later, ask a few of them, privately, whether getting what they needed felt easy and dignified. That one answer tells you more about your school than any test score.

Sit with this

Think of a school you went to. What small thing made you feel wanted, or unwanted, and did anyone in charge know?
