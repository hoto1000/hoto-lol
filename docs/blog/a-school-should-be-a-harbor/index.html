What should school feel like?

A school should be a harbor

Oct 24, 2026 · 4 min read

A lot of what makes school feel safe or unsafe comes down to small moments. Can a student take a snack without being watched, or sit somewhere without getting in trouble? Does the lunch line announce who owes money? Students may forget a policy, but they remember whether they felt wanted.

I am training as a family therapist, so I think about a school the way I think about a home. Rules matter, and so does the feeling underneath them. These are eight ideas a school could try, each with a practical change that does not need a big budget or a new law. A student should not have to explain or prove why they need any of them.

1. A school should be a harbor

A sorting machine ranks students as they come in. A harbor receives them as they arrive.

A calm welcome at the door, one adult every student knows by name and can go to, and a simple way to catch up after missing class could help a school feel more welcoming. When students miss a day, they should have a way to catch up without losing their standing.

2. Help without a spotlight

Some students need school supplies, hygiene items, or a snack. They may go without if asking in front of other people feels uncomfortable.

A school could put those things somewhere students can take them without asking, filling out a form, or proving need. It could be a shelf, a drawer, or a corner of the nurse's office. Then students could be asked privately whether the setup feels private and dignified, and the school could fix what they say is off.

This is the change I would start with. It is small, caring, and easy to test.

3. A soft landing before the first bell

A school could offer students a few optional, ungraded minutes at the start of the day. They could use that time to settle, read, or sit near an adult they trust.

There would be no forced check-in and no sharing circle. Adults would not ask how students are doing unless they bring it up. The aim is to let the day begin gently, without searching for what is wrong.

4. Quiet is part of belonging

Students could help design a calm, accessible space that anyone can use for a short while. No one would need to give a reason for being there.

Two boundaries would keep the space honest. It should never be a punishment room or a stand-in for real help when someone needs more than quiet. If the same student is there every day, that gives a caring adult information to act on, and the space should stay available.

5. The rules should hear the people who live under them

A school could choose one everyday policy, such as phones, hallway passes, or late work. Students could help shape it.

Adults could explain up front what can change, what cannot, and why. Then they could show students how their input affected the final decision, or did not, and be honest about the reasons. Students can handle a no. Being asked and then ignored is harder.

6. Teach peace as a skill

Listening, repairing a conflict, and disagreeing without humiliating someone all take practice. Nobody is born knowing how to do them.

Students could have optional practice that is short, low stakes, and never used as discipline. The aim is for students to know what to do after a fight, whatever the discipline records say.

7. The lunch table is part of the social contract

A student should be able to eat without being shamed for a balance their family owes. That debt should not become part of the meal.

A school could make nourishing meals easy to get and end lunch debt shaming in every form, from stamps on hands to taking a tray away. Students could also have a real say in what is served. When a school feeds students without conditions, it says something about their worth.

8. A library should feel like a second living room

A school could keep the library open beyond class hours. Students could find books, internet, quiet, and an adult who helps without asking why they need it.

For some students, the library is the only calm, warm, free place with Wi-Fi in their whole week. Running it with that in mind matters.

What these have in common

Each idea protects privacy and choice. Students do not have to explain themselves or join in. A school could treat each one as a small experiment in dignity and belonging, try it for a semester, and ask students privately how it felt. It can keep what students say works and drop what they say does not.

I do not know how to make a whole school feel like a harbor, and I am not sure anyone does. I want to start with the shelf.

Where to start this week

A school could start with help without a spotlight. Pick one shelf, stock it, and tell students it is there and nobody will ask why they used it. A month later, ask a few students privately whether getting what they needed felt easy and dignified. That answer tells you more about the school than any test score.

The argument

  1. 1. A school should be a harbor
  2. 2. Help without a spotlight
  3. 3. A soft landing before the first bell
  4. 4. Quiet is part of belonging
  5. 5. The rules should hear the people who live under them
  6. 6. Teach peace as a skill
  7. 7. The lunch table is part of the social contract
  8. 8. A library should feel like a second living room

Sit with this

Think of a school you went to. What small thing made you feel wanted, or unwanted, and did anyone in charge know?
