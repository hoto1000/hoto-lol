Map your social location

A quiet page for thinking about where you stand in the world. Nothing here is typed in or saved, and there's no score. You can also print it and write on paper.

Your social location is the mix of groups you belong to, like your age, your culture, your class, and your gender. Where you stand shapes what you can see easily, and what's hard to see.

Therapists in training are asked to map their own before they try to understand anyone else's. These questions come from what I'm learning in my program. Go slowly, and skip anything that doesn't feel right today.

Part 1: The parts of you

Psychologist Pamela Hays made a list called ADDRESSING. Each letter is one part of who we are. Read each question and notice what comes up.

  1. A · Age and generationWhat was happening in the world while you grew up, and how did it shape what feels normal to you?
  2. D · Developmental disabilityGrowing up, were the places around you built for the way your body and mind work?
  3. D · Disability later in lifeHas an illness, injury, or change in your body or mind changed how the world treats you?
  4. R · Religion and spiritualityWhat did your family believe, and what do you believe now?
  5. E · Ethnic and racial identityHow do you describe your ethnicity and race? How do other people describe it?
  6. S · Socioeconomic statusGrowing up, did money feel steady or uncertain? What about now?
  7. S · Sexual orientationWhen have you felt free, or not free, to be open about who you love?
  8. I · Indigenous heritageDo you have Indigenous roots? Whose land do you live on now?
  9. N · National originWhere were you born, where were your parents born, and what language did you hear at home?
  10. G · GenderWhat did people expect of you because of your gender?

Now look back over your answers. Which parts do you think about almost every day? Which ones rarely cross your mind?

The parts we rarely think about are often the ones where the world was already built for us. Peggy McIntosh wrote about this in 1989 as a kind of invisible backpack of advantages that people don't notice they're carrying.

A question to sit with

Pick one part you rarely think about. What might someone who stands in a different place on that part have to think about every day?

Part 2: Where the parts overlap

In 1989, law professor Kimberlé Crenshaw gave a name to something people had lived for a long time. She called it intersectionality. It means the parts of us overlap, and the overlap can matter more than any one part alone.

She pointed to a 1976 case where Black women sued General Motors. The court said there was no race discrimination because GM hired Black men, and no sex discrimination because GM hired white women. The women who were both Black and women fell through the gap.

Questions to sit with

Has there been a time when two parts of you together changed how you were treated, more than either one would alone?

Which of your parts seem to get noticed first by other people? Which ones stay hidden?

Part 3: The circles around you

Psychologist Urie Bronfenbrenner pictured a person in the middle of circles. Family and friends are closest. Farther out are things like a parent's job and the local school board. On the outside are culture and laws. Later he added one more layer, time itself.

I wrote more about his circles in Who raises a kid?

Closest circle

Who was around you every day growing up? Who is around you now?

The circles you never saw

What decisions shaped your life that you weren't in the room for, like a parent's work schedule or a school's budget?

The outer circle

What did your culture and your country tell you a good life looks like? Do you still believe it?

Time

What big events happened during your life, and how old were you when they did?

After you finish

Some of these questions can bring up heavy things. It's okay to stop. If you're in crisis, you can call or text 988.

This page is for reflection. It isn't a test or a diagnosis, and it can't tell you who you are. Only you can do that, and your answers can change over time.
