Think about the last form that asked you to describe yourself in checkboxes. Good, fair, poor. Normal, elevated, high. The boxes make those lines look settled, as if someone already knew where each one belonged.

Foucault's early work on madness

Foucault's first major book, History of Madness (1961), follows one category as it changes shape across centuries. In the Renaissance, madness had a kind of voice. It could be strange and unsettling, and at times people treated it as a form of insight. Then came the great confinement in the 1600s. People called mad were locked away alongside the poor and the idle. Later, medicine made madness a condition to study, diagnose, and treat.

Foucault did not claim that nothing was ever wrong with anyone. He argued that “madness” was no fixed object waiting to be discovered. The institutions doing the confining kept drawing the category again. That category shaped who got heard, who got locked up, and who had the authority to set the terms.

The medical gaze

In The Birth of the Clinic (1963), Foucault describes a change in the way medicine looks at people. The patient can fade from view as a person with a story and become a case, a body to read, a set of signs to classify. He called this way of seeing the medical gaze.

That gaze has a purpose. It helps doctors learn to see patterns quickly enough to help. At the same time, it remains one way of looking, and every way of looking has blind spots. I keep thinking about what can be missed when someone does not fit the pattern.

Discipline and the norm

Discipline and Punish (1975) brings the discussion closer to ordinary life. I covered the panopticon in an earlier post, and norms are the other half of that book.

Foucault argued that modern institutions punish what is wrong and also produce a standard for what counts as normal. Then they measure everyone against it. Schools rank. Clinics classify. Workplaces evaluate. As the measurements become familiar, they can start to seem like plain descriptions of reality, and the decisions behind them can fade from view.

I see that as a bridge between the norm and the label.

Power/knowledge

This is the idea that connects the earlier parts of Foucault's work. He did not treat knowledge as neutral information sitting in a library. He argued that knowledge and power are produced together. Whoever has the authority to name something also shapes how it is seen, studied, and treated.

A diagnosis carries knowledge, and it can also involve power. In a plain sense, a person with credentials has the authority to decide what to call what another person is living through.

The part people get wrong

Foucault is often read as saying medicine is a lie or diagnosis is oppression. That reading misses his argument and, to me, does little to help anyone understand it.

Diagnoses can be lifesaving. They can open doors to care, insurance, accommodations, and community. A diagnosis can give someone a word for something they carried alone for years. Naming a pattern can bring relief.

His point is more specific. People make labels in history and for particular reasons. Since people make them, it is fair to ask what a label does and whether it is accurate.

When the label becomes the self

The risk can be hard to notice. Once a category exists, it can begin to take up too much space around the person it describes.

A diagnosis describes one part of a person. When every appointment, form, and conversation runs through the label, it can become easy to tell your whole life in its language. Foucault worried that sorting happens so smoothly people may stop noticing it, whether or not they were sorted correctly.

Norms also sort people into normal and not, and being placed on the wrong side of that line can affect how others treat you. That weight comes from the social world around a person, and it deserves its own name.

So what do you do with this

Keep the care and ask better questions.

When a label lands, I think this question is worth sitting with: “who decided this is where the line goes, what was that decision built on, and what does the label do to how I see myself?” A diagnosis can be useful while you keep some distance from it.

Notice the parts of you the category does not cover, too. A category is like a map, and maps leave out most of the territory.

One thing to try this week

Choose one label you carry, a diagnosis, a type, a personality result, or whatever sits in your file. Write down two lists.

For the first list, write what the label has given you: access, words, treatment, understanding, community.

For the second, write what the label has never described. Include the parts of you that do not fit, the days it does not explain, and the things you would still be if the label vanished tomorrow.

Then read the second list again. I want to keep asking what else belongs in a story when a label starts to feel like the whole thing.
