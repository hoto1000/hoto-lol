Verdict: labels make messy lives look neat. Forms offer boxes: good, fair, poor. Normal, elevated, high. The lines look settled. Someone picked where each one belongs.

Foucault's early work on madness

Foucault's first major book, History of Madness (1961), follows a category across centuries. In the Renaissance, madness could have a strange, unsettling voice, sometimes treated as insight. During the great confinement in the 1600s, people called mad were locked away with the poor and idle. Later, medicine made madness a condition to study, diagnose, and treat.

Foucault did not claim that nothing was ever wrong with anyone. He argued that “madness” was no fixed object waiting to be discovered. Institutions redrew the category, shaping who got heard, locked up, and given authority to set the terms.

The medical gaze

In The Birth of the Clinic (1963), Foucault describes medicine's changing view of people. The patient's story can fade; the patient can become a case, a body to read, and a set of signs to classify. He called this way of seeing the medical gaze.

That gaze has a purpose. It helps doctors learn to see patterns quickly enough to help. It remains one way of looking. Every way of looking has blind spots. I keep thinking about what gets missed when someone falls outside the pattern.

Discipline and the norm

Discipline and Punish (1975) brings the discussion closer to ordinary life. I covered the panopticon earlier. Norms are the other half of that book.

Foucault argued modern institutions punish what is wrong and produce standards for what counts as normal, then measure people against them. Schools rank, clinics classify, workplaces evaluate. Familiar measurements can look like plain descriptions of reality while the choices behind them fade from view.

I see that as a bridge between the norm and the label.

Power/knowledge

This ties earlier themes together: Foucault connected knowledge and power, arguing they are produced together. Whoever can name something shapes how it is seen, studied, and treated.

A diagnosis carries knowledge and can involve power. Credentials give someone authority to name what another person is living through.

The part people get wrong

People often read Foucault as saying medicine is a lie or diagnosis is oppression. That misses his argument. To me, it does little to help anyone understand it.

Diagnoses can be lifesaving. They can open doors to care, insurance, accommodations, and community. A diagnosis can give someone a word for something they carried alone for years. Naming a pattern can bring relief.

His point is more specific. People make labels in history and for particular reasons. We can ask what a label does and whether it is accurate.

Shared categories matter too. On 25 May 2019, WHO reported Member States agreed to adopt ICD-11, effective 1 January 2022. WHO calls the ICD the international standard for reporting diseases and health conditions, and says it supports clinical records, research, patient safety, reimbursement, and health-resource decisions. Common categories make patterns comparable and help plan services, while each code still describes only part of a person. WHO's 25 May 2019 World Health Assembly update.

When the label becomes the self

The risk can be hard to notice. Once a category exists, it can take up too much space around the person it describes.

A diagnosis describes one part of a person. When every appointment, form, and conversation runs through the label, it can become easy to tell your whole life in its language. Foucault worried that sorting happens so smoothly people may stop noticing it, whether or not they were sorted correctly.

Norms also sort people into normal and not. Being placed on the wrong side of that line can affect how others treat you. That weight comes from the social world around a person. It deserves its own name.

So what do you do with this

Keep the care. Question how the label is being used.

When a label lands, I think this question is worth asking: “who decided this is where the line goes, what was that decision built on, and what does the label do to how I see myself?” A diagnosis can be useful. Keep some distance from it.

Notice what the category leaves out. A category is like a map. Maps leave out most of the territory.

What I still don't know

Which parts of a diagnosis feel accurate to the person who receives it? How can a clinician leave room for that person's account when a form asks for one category? What would make it easy to revisit a label after circumstances change?

One thing to try this week

Choose one label you carry, a diagnosis, type, personality result, or something in your file. Write two lists. First, what the label has given you: access, words, treatment, understanding, community. Second, what it has never described: parts that do not fit, days it does not explain, and things you would still be if it vanished tomorrow.

Then read the second list again. I want to keep asking what else belongs in a story when a label starts to feel like the whole thing.
