Verdict: labels make messy lives look neat. Forms offer boxes: good, fair, poor. Normal, elevated, high. The lines look settled. Someone picked where each one belongs.

Foucault's early work on madness

Foucault's first major book, History of Madness (1961), follows one category as it changes shape across centuries. In the Renaissance, madness had a kind of voice. It could be strange and unsettling. At times, people treated it as a form of insight. Then came the great confinement in the 1600s. People called mad were locked away alongside the poor and the idle. Later, medicine made madness a condition to study, diagnose, and treat.

Foucault did not claim that nothing was ever wrong with anyone. He argued that “madness” was no fixed object waiting to be discovered. The institutions doing the confining kept drawing the category again. That category shaped who got heard, who got locked up, and who had the authority to set the terms.

The medical gaze

In The Birth of the Clinic (1963), Foucault describes a change in the way medicine looks at people. The patient can fade from view as a person with a story. The patient can become a case, a body to read, a set of signs to classify. He called this way of seeing the medical gaze.

That gaze has a purpose. It helps doctors learn to see patterns quickly enough to help. It remains one way of looking. Every way of looking has blind spots. I keep thinking about what gets missed when someone falls outside the pattern.

Discipline and the norm

Discipline and Punish (1975) brings the discussion closer to ordinary life. I covered the panopticon earlier. Norms are the other half of that book.

Foucault argued that modern institutions punish what is wrong and also produce a standard for what counts as normal. Then they measure everyone against it. Schools rank. Clinics classify. Workplaces evaluate. The measurements become familiar. They can start to seem like plain descriptions of reality. The decisions behind them can fade from view.

I see that as a bridge between the norm and the label.

Power/knowledge

This ties together the earlier parts of Foucault's work. He tied knowledge to power. He argued that knowledge and power are produced together. Whoever has the authority to name something also shapes how it is seen, studied, and treated.

A diagnosis carries knowledge and can involve power. In plain terms, a person with credentials has the authority to name what another person is living through.

The part people get wrong

People often read Foucault as saying medicine is a lie or diagnosis is oppression. That misses his argument. To me, it does little to help anyone understand it.

Diagnoses can be lifesaving. They can open doors to care, insurance, accommodations, and community. A diagnosis can give someone a word for something they carried alone for years. Naming a pattern can bring relief.

His point is more specific. People make labels in history and for particular reasons. We can ask what a label does and whether it is accurate.

When the label becomes the self

The risk can be hard to notice. Once a category exists, it can take up too much space around the person it describes.

A diagnosis describes one part of a person. When every appointment, form, and conversation runs through the label, it can become easy to tell your whole life in its language. Foucault worried that sorting happens so smoothly people may stop noticing it, whether or not they were sorted correctly.

Norms also sort people into normal and not. Being placed on the wrong side of that line can affect how others treat you. That weight comes from the social world around a person. It deserves its own name.

So what do you do with this

Keep the care. Question how the label is being used.

When a label lands, I think this question is worth asking: “who decided this is where the line goes, what was that decision built on, and what does the label do to how I see myself?” A diagnosis can be useful. Keep some distance from it.

Notice what the category leaves out. A category is like a map. Maps leave out most of the territory.

One thing to try this week

Choose one label you carry: a diagnosis, a type, a personality result, or whatever sits in your file. Write down two lists.

For the first list, write what the label has given you: access, words, treatment, understanding, community.

For the second, write what the label has never described. Include the parts of you that do not fit, the days it does not explain, and the things you would still be if the label vanished tomorrow.

Then read the second list again. I want to keep asking what else belongs in a story when a label starts to feel like the whole thing.
