In 1786, while visiting his brother Samuel in Russia, an English philosopher named Jeremy Bentham sketched plans for a prison he called the Panopticon. The basic idea was Samuel's. Jeremy turned it into a prison.

The design was simple. A ring of cells around a central tower. Each cell lit and open to the tower. One guard in the tower could look into any cell at any moment. The prisoners could never tell when they were being watched.

The clever part, and the cruel part, is that it did not matter whether the guard was actually there. Once people believed they might be seen, they started policing themselves. Bentham called it a “new mode of obtaining power of mind over mind.”

Foucault's point

Almost two hundred years later, Michel Foucault picked up that design in Discipline and Punish (1975) and made it the centerpiece of a much bigger argument.

Foucault was not really writing about prisons. He was asking how modern power works. His answer was that it works less through violence or public spectacle than through discipline. Schools, barracks, hospitals, factories, and prisons all use the same basic toolkit. Timetables, exams, ranks, files, and constant visibility.

The Panopticon was the perfect symbol for it. Visibility becomes a trap. You never know when the eye is on you, so you act as if it always is. Foucault's line: the inmate “is seen, but he does not see; he is the object of information, never a subject in communication.”

The important move is what happens inside the person. Power stops being something done to you and becomes something you do to yourself. Foucault called the result a docile body. Nobody was broken. People had simply learned to manage their own behavior in advance.

He went further too. Discipline also produces norms. It sorts people into normal and abnormal, healthy and unhealthy, employable and not. Those categories arrive feeling like plain facts about the world. Often they are the leftover of an institution deciding what to measure.

So what does this have to do with your phone

You are not in a cell. There is no tower. But the shape of a feed is oddly familiar.

Your profile is the cell. It is lit, it is legible, and it exists to be seen. The audience is the tower. You cannot see who is looking or when, and the number on a post is a rough proxy for attention that never quite tells you who the eye belongs to.

Algorithms do not just watch. They rank, sort, and predict. They decide whose post travels and whose disappears. That is measurement deciding what deserves to be noticed, which is very close to the norm-making Foucault described.

Then comes the part he would recognize best. You start doing the work for them.

You draft the caption three times. You check the post again at 11pm. You leave out the strange or unfinished thought because it will not perform. Nobody forced any of that. You did it because you have internalized the possibility of being watched.

That is the loop: self-censorship from within, with no censor above.

Two honest caveats

First, the analogy is not exact. Bentham's guard had authority. Your audience mostly does not. Likes are not a prison sentence, and Foucault was writing about institutions with real power to confine, classify, and treat people. The image is useful as a comparison and should not be read as a diagnosis of your feed.

Second, Foucault was not a self-help writer. He did not hand out five steps to a happier life. He wrote about how power circulates and how people can push back in small, specific ways. Critique, in his framing, is the art of not being governed quite so much.

That last idea is the useful one here. You cannot log out of power. But you can notice where the gaze has moved from the tower into your own head, and you can decide how much of your life it gets to author.

One thing to try this week

Pick one thing you would have posted, and do not post it. Say it to a friend, write it in a notebook, or just leave it in your head. You are not punishing yourself or quitting social media. You are noticing how much attention goes into anticipating the reaction. Watch what happens to the sentence when nobody is grading it.

If that feels too small, do the reverse. Post something deliberately unoptimized. No hook, no hashtags, no timing strategy. See if the sky falls.

Then notice one more thing while you scroll. Ask who is measuring what. Every feed is a set of judgments about what deserves to be seen, and you are allowed to have opinions about the judge.
