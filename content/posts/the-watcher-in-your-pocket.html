Verdict: The phone is a pocket-sized panopticon, with serious limits.

In 1786, English philosopher Jeremy Bentham visited his brother Samuel in Russia. He sketched a prison called the Panopticon. The basic idea came from Samuel. Jeremy turned it into a prison.

Cells formed a ring around a central tower. Each cell was lit and open to the tower. One guard could look into any cell at any moment. Prisoners could never tell when they were being watched.

That is the design's sharp trick, and its cruelty. The guard did not have to be there. People who believed they might be seen began policing themselves. Bentham called it a “new mode of obtaining power of mind over mind.”

Foucault's point

Almost two hundred years later, Michel Foucault took up the design in Discipline and Punish (1975). He used it to make a much larger argument.

He asked how modern power works. His answer was that it works more through discipline than through violence or public spectacle. Schools, barracks, hospitals, factories, and prisons share a basic set of tools: timetables, exams, ranks, files, and constant visibility.

The Panopticon became a clear symbol for that argument. Visibility can turn into a trap. You cannot tell when the eye is on you, so you act as if it is always there. Foucault described the inmate this way: “is seen, but he does not see; he is the object of information, never a subject in communication.”

The change happens inside the person. Power shifts from something done to you into something you do to yourself. Foucault called the result a docile body. People had learned to manage their own behavior ahead of time, without anyone having to break them.

Foucault also wrote about the norms that discipline produces. They sort people into normal and abnormal, healthy and unhealthy, employable and not. Those categories can feel like plain facts about the world, even when they come from an institution's decision about what to measure.

So what does this have to do with your phone

A social-media feed has a shape that reminded me of the Panopticon. You are not in a cell, and there is no tower. The comparison can still be useful.

Your profile is like the cell. It is lit, legible, and there to be seen. Your audience takes the place of the tower. You cannot see who is looking or when. The number on a post gives a rough proxy for attention, though it never tells you who the eye belongs to.

Algorithms do more than watch. They rank, sort, and predict. They decide whose post travels and whose disappears. Measurement helps determine what gets noticed, which connects with the norm-making Foucault described.

Then comes the part Foucault might recognize most: you start doing the work for them.

You might draft a caption three times, check the post again at 11pm, or leave out a strange or unfinished thought because it will not perform. Nobody forced those choices. You made them after taking in the possibility of being watched.

That is the loop: self-censorship from within, with no censor above.

Two honest caveats

The comparison has limits. Bentham's guard had authority, and your audience mostly does not. Likes carry no prison sentence. Foucault wrote about institutions with real power to confine, classify, and treat people. Use the image as a comparison. Do not read it as a diagnosis of your feed.

Foucault's writing does not offer five steps to a happier life. He wrote about how power circulates and how people can push back in small, specific ways. In his framing, critique is the art of not being governed quite so much.

That last idea feels useful here. You cannot log out of power. I want to keep in view where the gaze has moved from the tower into my own head. Then I can decide how much of my life it gets to author.

One thing to try this week

Choose one thing you would have posted and leave it unposted. Say it to a friend, write it in a notebook, or leave it in your head. This is a small experiment, not a punishment or a decision to quit social media. Notice how much attention goes into anticipating the reaction. See what happens to the sentence when nobody is grading it.

If that feels too small, try the reverse. Post something deliberately unoptimized. No hook, no hashtags, no timing strategy. See if the sky falls.

As you scroll, ask one more question: who is measuring what? Every feed makes judgments about what deserves to be seen. You are allowed to have opinions about the judge.
