During the spring of 1961, Adolf Eichmann sat in a glass booth in a Jerusalem courtroom and answered questions about the deaths of millions of people. He had run the logistics of the Nazi deportations. Israeli agents had captured him in Argentina the year before, and he was on trial for crimes against the Jewish people. Hannah Arendt, a philosopher, went to watch and wrote about the trial for The New Yorker. Two words from her coverage, “banality of evil,” have been quoted, misquoted, and argued over ever since.

What she saw in the courtroom

Many people expected a monster. Arendt described a man who could barely speak without falling back on stock phrases and clichés. The Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy summarizes her view this way: she saw a “clown” where many expected a “monster,” and linked his deeds in part to “thoughtlessness.” She meant an inability to look at anything from another person's point of view.

The book that followed, Eichmann in Jerusalem: A Report on the Banality of Evil, carried the phrase in its subtitle and used it once more near the end. Arendt explained the point later in a letter to the scholar Gershom Scholem. Evil, she wrote, is never “radical.” It is only extreme, and it can lay waste to the world because it spreads “like a fungus on the surface.”

What the phrase does not say

Some people take the phrase to mean that evil is boring, or that Eichmann was a harmless clerk who followed orders. Arendt's account says he pursued the deportations with zeal, and she supported his death sentence. She held him fully responsible for what he did.

The phrase describes one man's character and motives. It refers to how Eichmann appeared to Arendt in court, and the Stanford Encyclopedia notes that she never meant it as a general theory of Nazi evil.

The argument that followed

The criticism began right away, and some of it has held up. Many critics, including the historian Bettina Stangneth in Eichmann Before Jerusalem, argue that Arendt mistook a performance at trial for the real man. On that reading, Eichmann was a convinced antisemite who presented himself in court as a bureaucrat. Her book also drew anger for its tone and its claims about the Jewish Councils, bodies the Nazis forced Jewish communities to form.

I think both things can be true at once. One trial may have shown Arendt a face that was partly staged. Her larger question still stays with me: how do ordinary people take part in terrible things without feeling that they have decided anything?

Thinking as a habit

Arendt kept working on that question later in her career. She asked whether “the habit of examining whatever happens to come to pass” might be one of the conditions that keep people from doing evil. Her answer involved what she called thinking, the silent dialogue a person has with themselves.

In her essay on personal responsibility under dictatorship, she described the few people who refused to collaborate. They did not have firmer moral rules than their neighbors. They asked whether they could still live in peace with themselves after doing a certain deed. “I cannot walk away from myself,” she wrote.

That seems like a modest standard to me. It does not require heroism or a philosophy degree. It asks for a pause long enough to consider what an action is and who it lands on.

Why it still gets quoted

Arendt wrote about totalitarian rule, so comparisons need care. Most workplace shortcuts and bureaucratic habits are nowhere near the Final Solution. Still, I can see her observation carrying over in a small way to ordinary life. People repeat the phrases of their institution, follow the form, and tell themselves someone else owns the decision.

That kind of noticing takes practice. It can start with small things: a policy someone repeats without having read, or a line passed along because everyone else does. I want to catch the moment when a phrase starts doing the thinking for me.
