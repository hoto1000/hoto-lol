What did Hannah Arendt mean when she called evil banal?

The phrase is often flattened into a lazy excuse for stopping thought. In spring 1961, Adolf Eichmann sat in a glass booth in a Jerusalem courtroom, answering questions about the deaths of millions. He had run Nazi deportation logistics. Israeli agents captured him in Argentina the year before, and he stood trial for crimes against the Jewish people. Philosopher Hannah Arendt watched and reported on the trial for The New Yorker. The phrase “banality of evil” has been quoted, misquoted, and argued over ever since.

What she saw in the courtroom

Many expected a monster, which can make her point easy to miss. Arendt described a man who could barely speak without falling back on stock phrases and clichés. The Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy summarizes her view this way: she saw a “clown” where many expected a “monster,” and linked his deeds in part to “thoughtlessness.” She meant an inability to look at anything from another person's point of view.

The book that followed, Eichmann in Jerusalem: A Report on the Banality of Evil, carried the phrase in its subtitle and used it once more near the end. Arendt explained the point later in a letter to the scholar Gershom Scholem. Evil, she wrote, is never “radical.” It is only extreme, and it can lay waste to the world because it spreads “like a fungus on the surface.”

What the phrase does not say

Some people hear the phrase as meaning evil is boring or Eichmann was a harmless clerk who followed orders. Arendt's account says he pursued the deportations with zeal; she supported his death sentence and held him fully responsible for what he did.

The United States Holocaust Memorial Museum reports that Eichmann and his aides deported some 440,000 Hungarian Jews from late April to early July 1944. The Museum's account records the estimate and dates.

The phrase describes Eichmann's character and motives as he appeared to Arendt in court. The Stanford Encyclopedia notes she did not mean it as a general theory of Nazi evil.

The argument that followed

Criticism began right away, and some of it has held up. The strongest objection, made by historian Bettina Stangneth in Eichmann Before Jerusalem and others, is that Arendt mistook Eichmann's courtroom performance for his character, underweighted his antisemitism, and left his deliberate choices underexplained. Her book also drew anger for its tone and claims about the Jewish Councils, bodies the Nazis forced Jewish communities to form. The Stanford Encyclopedia outlines this criticism.

I think both things can be true at once. The trial may have shown Arendt a face that was partly staged. Her larger question stays with me: how do ordinary people take part in terrible things without feeling that they have decided anything?

Thinking as a habit

Arendt kept working on that question. She asked whether “the habit of examining whatever happens to come to pass” might help people refrain from evil. For her, thinking was a silent dialogue with oneself.

In her essay on personal responsibility under dictatorship, she described the few people who refused to collaborate. They did not have firmer moral rules than their neighbors. They asked whether they could still live in peace with themselves after doing a certain deed. “I cannot walk away from myself,” she wrote.

That seems like a modest standard to me. It does not require heroism or a philosophy degree. It asks for a pause long enough to consider what an action is and who it lands on.

Why it still gets quoted

Arendt wrote about totalitarian rule, so comparisons need care. Most workplace shortcuts and bureaucratic habits are nowhere near the Final Solution. I can still see her observation carrying over in a small way to ordinary life. People repeat the phrases of their institution, follow the form, and tell themselves someone else owns the decision.

That kind of noticing takes practice. It can start with small things: a policy someone repeats without having read, or a line passed along because everyone else does. I want to catch the moment when a phrase starts doing the thinking for me.
