For a long time I believed there was a number. If I earned enough, the stress would go away and I could finally relax. I think a lot of people my age carry a version of that number.

Growing up Asian American, money carried weight in my family. My grandfather did construction work, and my mom became a pharmacist. Every step up felt paid for by the generation before. When I started reading research on income and happiness, I wanted to know if the number was real.

Where the "$75,000" idea came from

In 2010, Daniel Kahneman and Angus Deaton analyzed more than 450,000 answers to a daily Gallup survey of Americans. They distinguished life evaluation, how people rate their lives overall, from emotional well-being, how their days feel, including joy, stress, sadness, or anger.

Life evaluation kept rising as income went up. Emotional well-being rose too, but past about $75,000 a year it stopped improving in their data. That finding became a headline people still repeat: money buys happiness up to $75,000, and then it stops.

The Census Bureau reported real median household income of $87,460 for 2025. Its release, published September 15, 2026, counts pretax money income and excludes in-kind transfers. That household statistic isn't a personal target, and it leaves room for differences in family size and cost of living. The Census Bureau's 2025 income release explains what the estimate measures.

The number moved

In 2018, Andrew Jebb's Purdue-led team used the Gallup World Poll, a sample of more than 1.7 million people. Their worldwide leveling points were about $95,000 for life evaluation and $60,000 to $75,000 for emotional well-being. Those amounts are for one person and likely higher for families. The point came later in wealthier regions; in some areas, people past it rated their lives a little lower.

That made me realize a "perfect income" depends a lot on where you live and what the people around you have.

Then someone found no plateau at all

In 2021, Matthew Killingsworth at Penn collected about 1.7 million in-the-moment check-ins from 33,391 working adults in the US, asking how they felt right then. Both kinds of happiness kept rising with income, with no plateau above $75,000.

Two careful studies disagreed. I liked that the researchers worked it out together: Kahneman and Killingsworth teamed with Barbara Mellers to reanalyze the data.

Both were right, for different people

Their 2023 paper found happiness kept climbing with income for most people. For the least happy 15 to 20 percent, it rose up to about $100,000, the old threshold adjusted for inflation, then mostly flattened. At the happiest end, it climbed faster above $100,000.

To me, money can fix problems that make life miserable: rent, debt, a car that keeps breaking down, or trouble seeing a doctor. Kahneman and Deaton also found low income makes divorce, illness, and being alone hurt more. Once those money problems are handled, what remains for that unhappy group may be grief, loneliness, or depression.

I want to be honest that this isn't settled. A fair counterargument is that observed patterns don't prove income caused well-being to change; in 2024, researchers questioned both the strength of the flattening and the causal story. I hold the $100,000 number loosely, though the broader pattern still seems consistent.

Why it levels off

Money works on a curve. In these studies, happiness tracks percentage change in income better than dollar change. Going from $30,000 to $60,000 doubles income; $200,000 to $230,000 adds the same dollars but only a 15 percent raise, which feels much smaller.

We get used to things. Psychologists call this hedonic adaptation. A raise or nicer apartment feels great for a while, then becomes normal. Jebb's team saw signs of adaptation and of wants growing with income.

We compare ourselves to the people around us. Erzo Luttmer found that, at the same personal income, people with higher-earning neighbors reported being less happy. This fits the higher leveling-off point in wealthier regions: when people around you earn more, "enough" goes up too.

Some of what shapes a good day has little to do with money. In Kahneman and Deaton's data, health, caregiving, loneliness, and smoking predicted daily emotions more strongly than income and education. Money can support those things, but can't stand in for them.

What this means for me

Looking back, the number I carried was mostly about safety. The research backs this up: getting out of real financial stress changes how life feels.

I'm trying to unlearn the idea that the number keeps growing. As someone training to be a therapist, I want to remember that money can carry meanings of security, worth, and family expectations. I'm still working on separating those for myself.

What I still don't know

How much of the connection between income and well-being reflects direct relief from financial stress, and how much reflects other parts of life that move alongside income? What does "enough" mean when housing, health care, and family responsibilities differ?
