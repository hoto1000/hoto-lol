In my last post I looked at why happiness seems to level off as income goes up. Once the money problems are handled, more money does less. That left me with an obvious next question. If more money stops helping as much, what does help?

I'm a student, so I went back to the research. Three things kept showing up: time, people, and how we spend what we already have.

Time

In 2016, Hal Hershfield, Cassie Mogilner and Uri Barnea asked thousands of Americans a simple question. Would you rather have more money or more time? Most people picked money. The people who picked time were happier, even after the researchers accounted for how much time and money they already had.

That one surprised me, because I would have picked money too. Growing up, I learned that time was something you traded for money, and the trade usually felt worth it. My family worked hard so the next generation could have more. I still respect that. At the same time, I hadn't thought much about what it costs to always choose the paycheck.

A year later, Ashley Whillans and her colleagues found something that connects the two. Across more than 6,000 people in the US, Canada, Denmark and the Netherlands, people who spent money on time-saving services, like paying someone to clean or run errands, reported higher life satisfaction. In a field experiment, working adults felt happier after a time-saving purchase than after buying a material thing.

So money can buy happiness in a way. It just works better when it buys back your time.

People

The Harvard Study of Adult Development started in 1938 with 268 Harvard sophomores. Later it added 456 young men from inner-city Boston, and eventually their wives. It is one of the longest studies of adult life ever done.

The Harvard Gazette's write-up of the study put the main finding simply: close relationships, more than money or fame, were what kept people happy across their lives. As the study's director, Robert Waldinger, put it, "The people who were the most satisfied in their relationships at age 50 were the healthiest at age 80." That held true for both the Harvard men and the inner-city group.

I think about this a lot as someone training to be a therapist. When people come in stressed about money, the money is real. Underneath it, there are often questions about family, belonging, and who they can lean on. I want to remember to ask about both.

How we spend what we have

Two more studies changed how I think about spending.

In 2008, Elizabeth Dunn, Lara Aknin and Michael Norton found that people who spent more of their money on others were happier. When they randomly gave people money and told them to spend it on someone else, those people ended up happier than the ones told to spend it on themselves.

Back in 2003, Leaf Van Boven and Thomas Gilovich found that experiences, like a trip or a concert, tended to make people happier than material things. One reason they gave is that experiences are more often shared with other people, and they become part of who we are.

I'm pretty frugal. I buy in bulk at Costco and try to stretch what I have. Reading this didn't make me want to spend more. It made me want to think more about where the money goes. A dinner with my brother probably does more for me than another thing I'll stop noticing in a month.

What I'm taking from this

Looking back at both posts, I think the honest answer is that money matters a lot until your basic needs and safety are covered. After that, more of it keeps helping, but less, and how you use it starts to matter more than how much you have.

I want to try a small experiment on myself. The next time I have a little extra, I want to spend it on time or on someone else, and see how it actually feels. I'll probably write about how it goes.
