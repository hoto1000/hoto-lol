What can a word that came out wrong really tell us?

Verdict first: a Freudian slip usually tells you less than people think. I first heard about it as a word that comes out before I plan it, maybe from a part of the mind I can't see. I've had that happen. I said something that didn't match what I meant, and it didn't feel like a statement about who I am. So I went and read what Freud claimed and what research says now.

What Freud said

In 1901, Freud wrote The Psychopathology of Everyday Life. Freud called slips faulty actions, claiming they let repressed unconscious wishes leak out. Linguist Mark Liberman collected examples on the blog Language Log. The president of the Austrian Parliament announced, “I take notice that a full quorum of members is present and herewith declare the sitting closed!” A member of the House of Commons named the honourable member for “Central Hell” instead of Hull. A woman tried to compliment her friend's hat and said, “I am sure you must have thrown this delightful hat together,” when she meant sewn.

Funny examples. Freud's theory changed over time. His earlier model split the mind into conscious, preconscious, and unconscious; his later one used the id, ego, and superego, while both models stayed in his thinking. The id seeks satisfaction now, the ego deals with reality, and the superego is the internalized voice of parents and society.

What researchers found later

Language Log notes that people make about one slip for every thousand words they speak. Speaking is a hard job for the brain, and many slips look like errors made while it builds a sentence. Psychologist Michael Motley tested this. With fake electrodes and the threat of a mild shock, people's slips leaned toward electrical words. In a sexual context, the slips leaned that way. So what's on your mind can tip a slip. The same experiment showed that slips are easy to produce for plain sound reasons.

Mick Power summarized later research for the British Psychological Society in 2000. Modern research supports much behavior running automatically outside awareness, but not Freud's instinctual energy base. It also questions how much unconscious activity comes from repression. Freud relied mainly on therapy sessions, which are hard to test from outside.

A fair counterargument is that context can shape slips: in a 1979 experiment, Motley and Baars found more electricity-related slips in a shock-focused context and more sex-related slips in a sex-focused one. This supports a context effect, though it cannot show what an individual slip means. Their study abstract.

What stays with us from early life

People develop ideas about themselves and the world that can stick. Psychologists call these schemas. The American Psychological Association defines a schema as a collection of basic knowledge guiding perception and interpretation; it notes that negative schemas, such as “I am a damaged person,” can come from early childhood experiences.

Attachment theory is related but separate. John Bowlby developed it, and Mary Ainsworth expanded it. It describes how infant-caregiver relationships shape later emotional development. Bowlby's internal working model is an APA-defined mental picture of the self, other people, and relationships, formed through early caregiving. That looks like the closest link to what I grew up with. I haven't read enough yet to say more than that.

What I want to try

Next time a word comes out wrong, I want to check the boring explanations first. Was I tired? Was I rushing? The reading makes me think those cover most slips. I'll keep paying attention and see what I notice.
