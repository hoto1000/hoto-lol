A note before we begin: I am a student sharing what I am learning. This is not therapy and it does not diagnose or treat anything.

The first thing I ever heard about Freud was the Freudian slip. The idea is that a word sometimes comes out of my mouth that I never planned to say, and that it may come from a part of my mind I cannot see directly. I have noticed this in myself. Now and then I say something that does not match what I meant, and it does not seem to say anything about who I actually am as a person. That made me curious about what the unconscious really is, so I looked up what Freud claimed and what researchers have found since.

What Freud said

Freud wrote about slips in his 1901 book The Psychopathology of Everyday Life. He called them faulty actions, and he believed they come from repressed, unconscious wishes. Language Log, a linguistics blog, lists several of the examples he gave in lecture notes by the linguist Mark Liberman. The president of the Austrian Parliament said, “I take notice that a full quorum of members is present and herewith declare the sitting closed!” A member of the House of Commons referred to the honourable member for “Central Hell” instead of Hull. A woman meant to compliment a friend's hat and said, “I am sure you must have thrown this delightful hat together,” when she meant to say sewn.

Freud's picture of the mind changed over his career. His first model divided it into the conscious, the preconscious, and the unconscious. His later model used the id, the ego, and the superego, and he did not fully abandon the first model. In that later model, the id pushes for immediate satisfaction, the ego negotiates with reality, and the superego is the internalized voice of parents and society.

What researchers found later

The same Language Log post points out that people make about one slip of the tongue for every thousand words they say. Speaking is a hard motor and memory task, and many slips seem to come from the way the brain builds sentences. The psychologist Michael Motley and his colleagues ran an experiment that tested this. When he set up an electrical context, with fake electrodes and the threat of a mild shock, the slips people made leaned toward electrical words. When he set up a sexual context, the slips leaned that way. So what is on your mind can tip a slip one way. The same experiment also showed that slips are easy to cause for purely sound-based reasons.

Mick Power, writing for the British Psychological Society in 2000, gives a broader summary. Modern research supports the idea that a lot of behavior runs automatically, outside awareness. It does not support Freud's idea of an instinctual energy base, and it questions how much of the unconscious comes from repression. Freud also relied mainly on what happened in therapy sessions, which is hard to test from the outside.

What stays with us from early life

I also think about how people grow up with certain ideas about themselves and the world, and how those ideas stay. Psychologists call these schemas. The American Psychological Association defines a schema as a collection of basic knowledge that guides how a person perceives and interprets things, and notes that negative schemas, such as “I am a damaged person,” can arise from early childhood experiences.

Attachment theory is close to this, but it is its own thing. Developed by John Bowlby and expanded by Mary Ainsworth, it describes how the relationships between infants and caregivers influence later emotional development. Bowlby also described what he called an internal working model, which the APA defines as a mental picture of the self, of other people, and of how relationships work, formed through early caregiving. That seems to be the closest link between what I grew up with and what stays with me. I would like to read more before saying anything firmer.

What I want to try

The next time a word slips out, I want to ask one question. Was I tired or rushing, or was something else on my mind? After reading this, I think the first answer is probably true more often than the second, and I would like to pay attention long enough to find out.
