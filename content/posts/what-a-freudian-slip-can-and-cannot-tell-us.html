A note before we begin: I am a student sharing what I am learning. This is not therapy and it does not diagnose or treat anything.

My first introduction to Freud was the Freudian slip. I understood it as a word coming out before I planned to say it, possibly from a part of the mind I cannot see directly. I've noticed this in myself from time to time. I say something that doesn't match what I meant, though I don't take it as a clear statement about who I am. That question made me curious about what the unconscious means, so I read about Freud's claims and later research.

What Freud said

In 1901, Freud wrote about slips in The Psychopathology of Everyday Life. He called them faulty actions and understood them as expressions of repressed, unconscious wishes. The linguistics blog Language Log has a post by linguist Mark Liberman that gathers several examples from Freud's lecture notes. In one, the president of the Austrian Parliament announced, “I take notice that a full quorum of members is present and herewith declare the sitting closed!” A member of the House of Commons referred to the honourable member for “Central Hell” instead of Hull. A woman tried to compliment her friend's hat and said, “I am sure you must have thrown this delightful hat together,” when she meant sewn.

Freud's account of the mind changed over his career. His earlier model separated the conscious, the preconscious, and the unconscious. His later model described the id, the ego, and the superego, while the earlier model remained part of his thinking. In this later account, the id seeks immediate satisfaction, the ego negotiates with reality, and the superego reflects the internalized voice of parents and society.

What researchers found later

The same Language Log post notes that people make about one slip of the tongue for every thousand words they speak. Speaking involves demanding motor and memory tasks, and many slips seem to happen as the brain puts sentences together. Psychologist Michael Motley and his colleagues tested this in an experiment. In an electrical context, with fake electrodes and the threat of a mild shock, people's slips leaned toward electrical words. In a sexual context, the slips leaned in that direction. What is on someone's mind can tip a slip one way. The experiment also showed that slips are easy to produce for purely sound-based reasons.

In a broader summary for the British Psychological Society in 2000, Mick Power described what later research supports. Modern research supports the idea that a lot of behavior happens automatically, outside awareness. It does not support Freud's idea of an instinctual energy base, and it raises questions about how much of the unconscious comes from repression. Freud also relied mainly on what happened in therapy sessions, which is difficult to test from the outside.

What stays with us from early life

I think about how people grow up with ideas about themselves and the world, and how those ideas can stay with them. Psychologists call these schemas. The American Psychological Association defines a schema as a collection of basic knowledge that guides how a person perceives and interprets things. It also notes that negative schemas, such as “I am a damaged person,” can arise from early childhood experiences.

Attachment theory is related, though it is a separate idea. John Bowlby developed it, and Mary Ainsworth expanded it. The theory describes how relationships between infants and caregivers influence later emotional development. Bowlby also wrote about an internal working model, which the APA defines as a mental picture of the self, of other people, and of how relationships work, formed through early caregiving. That seems like the closest connection between what I grew up with and what stays with me. I want to read more before drawing a firmer conclusion.

What I want to try

When a word comes out differently from what I intended, I want to pause and ask whether I was tired or rushing, or whether something else was on my mind. Reading this makes me think tiredness or rushing may explain it more often than the other possibility. I want to pay attention over time and see what I notice.
