A note before we begin: I am a student sharing what I am learning. This is not therapy and it does not diagnose or treat anything.

Verdict first: a Freudian slip usually tells you less than people think. I first heard about it as a word that comes out before I plan it, maybe from a part of the mind I can't see. I've had that happen. I said something that didn't match what I meant, and it didn't feel like a statement about who I am. So I went and read what Freud claimed and what research says now.

What Freud said

In 1901, Freud wrote The Psychopathology of Everyday Life. He called slips faulty actions and said they were repressed, unconscious wishes leaking out. Linguist Mark Liberman collected some of Freud's examples on the blog Language Log. The president of the Austrian Parliament announced, “I take notice that a full quorum of members is present and herewith declare the sitting closed!” A member of the House of Commons named the honourable member for “Central Hell” instead of Hull. A woman tried to compliment her friend's hat and said, “I am sure you must have thrown this delightful hat together,” when she meant sewn.

Funny examples. Freud's theory also kept changing. His earlier model split the mind into conscious, preconscious, and unconscious. His later model used the id, the ego, and the superego, and the earlier one stayed in his thinking. The id wants satisfaction now. The ego deals with reality. The superego is the internalized voice of parents and society.

What researchers found later

Language Log notes that people make about one slip for every thousand words they speak. Speaking is a hard job for the brain, and many slips look like errors made while it builds a sentence. Psychologist Michael Motley tested this. With fake electrodes and the threat of a mild shock, people's slips leaned toward electrical words. In a sexual context, the slips leaned that way. So what's on your mind can tip a slip. The same experiment showed that slips are easy to produce for plain sound reasons.

Mick Power summed up the later research for the British Psychological Society in 2000. Modern research supports the idea that a lot of behavior runs automatically, outside awareness. It does not support Freud's idea of an instinctual energy base. It also questions how much of the unconscious comes from repression. And Freud relied mainly on what happened in therapy sessions, which is hard to test from the outside.

What stays with us from early life

People grow up with ideas about themselves and the world, and those ideas can stick. Psychologists call them schemas. The American Psychological Association defines a schema as a collection of basic knowledge that guides how a person perceives and interprets things. It also notes that negative schemas, such as “I am a damaged person,” can come from early childhood experiences.

Attachment theory is related and separate. John Bowlby developed it, and Mary Ainsworth expanded it. It describes how infant and caregiver relationships shape later emotional development. Bowlby also wrote about the internal working model, which the APA defines as a mental picture of the self, of other people, and of how relationships work, formed through early caregiving. That looks like the closest link to what I grew up with. I haven't read enough yet to say more than that.

What I want to try

Next time a word comes out wrong, I want to check the boring explanations first. Was I tired? Was I rushing? The reading makes me think those cover most slips. I'll keep paying attention and see what I notice.
