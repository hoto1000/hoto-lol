In my house, nobody said "I love you" very much. My mom cooked. My dad quietly fixed things around the house. If someone was upset, it usually got brushed off and dinner went on.

It took me a long time to see that as a pattern, and even longer to understand where it came from.

The house I grew up in

My parents have been married about twenty-five years. I grew up with them and my siblings under one roof. We were always together, though not always close emotionally. My mom worked full time, cooked, cleaned, and carried most of the emotional weight at home. My dad was quiet and disciplined.

There was love. It showed up in actions. My mom's way of caring was food and acts of service, especially at family meals that pulled everybody to the same table. My siblings and I have always been close, and they've been my rocks through a lot.

Where it came from

My parents are both immigrants from Vietnam. They came from a culture that values discipline, humility, and hard work. Emotions can look like a distraction from getting through things, so they taught us to keep our heads down and not complain.

That made me independent and guarded. Faith gave our home structure, community, and a real sense of service and forgiveness. It also brought pressure to appear composed, so emotions were managed quietly.

Food and acts of service can be real care while feelings stay unspoken. Context helps me understand my parents' habits, but it doesn't erase how the silence felt to me.

I don't blame my parents. Their way of caring made sense given what they had been through and what they were trying to give us.

What changed after I moved

I live in San Diego, far from Philadelphia. I feel more Americanized than I used to, but still pulled toward my Vietnamese roots. Away from home, Vietnamese food became a reminder of love and connection. My grandparents' stories and advice keep me grounded in what matters.

Christmas and Thanksgiving gatherings helped us connect in ways daily life didn't. Our family has also become more open over the years, and I'm grateful for that.

What I'm still working on

Knowing what it feels like to hold things in helps me connect with people who struggle to say what they feel. I'm still working on asking for help and trusting others with my emotions.

I used to see vulnerability as weakness. I don't anymore. I'd tell my younger self it's okay to speak up and he doesn't have to carry everything alone.

Why it matters to me as a future therapist

I want to work with immigrant families balancing culture, faith, and communication. My story is one version, and many Vietnamese American families look different, so I'll need to ask before I assume.

Try this yourself: think about how love was shown in your house when you were little. Who showed it, and how? Then ask whether that was the only way.

What I still don't know

What would my parents say they learned about showing love? How do I ask for help without feeling that I need to have all the words figured out first?
