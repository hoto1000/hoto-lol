In my house, nobody said "I love you" very much. My mom cooked. My dad quietly fixed things around the house. If someone was upset, it usually got brushed off and dinner went on.

It took me a long time to see that as a pattern, and even longer to understand where it came from.

The house I grew up in

My parents have been married about twenty-five years, and I grew up with them and my siblings under one roof. We were always together, but we weren't always close in the emotional sense. My mom worked full time, cooked, and cleaned, and she carried most of the emotional weight at home. My dad was quiet and disciplined.

There was love. It just showed up in actions. My mom's way of caring was food and acts of service, especially at family meals that pulled everybody to the same table. My siblings and I have always been close, and they've been my rocks through a lot.

Where it came from

My parents are both immigrants from Vietnam. They came from a culture that values discipline, humility, and hard work. In that world, emotions can look like a distraction from getting through things. So they taught us to keep our heads down and not complain.

That made me independent. It also made me guarded. Faith was a big part of our home too, and it gave us structure, community, and a real sense of service and forgiveness. It also came with some pressure to appear composed, so emotions were something to manage quietly.

I don't blame my parents for any of this. Their way of caring made sense given what they had been through and what they were trying to give us.

What changed after I moved

I live in San Diego now, a long way from Philadelphia. I feel more Americanized than I used to, but I still get pulled back toward my Vietnamese roots. Vietnamese food became a reminder of love and connection once I was far from home. My grandparents' stories and advice keep me grounded in what matters.

Family gatherings like Christmas and Thanksgiving gave us time to connect in ways that daily life didn't. Our family also became more open over the years, and I'm grateful for that.

What I'm still working on

I know what it feels like to hold things in. That helps me connect with people who struggle to say what they feel. At the same time, I'm still working on asking for help and trusting other people with my emotions.

I used to see vulnerability as weakness. I don't anymore. If I could talk to my younger self, I'd tell him it's okay to speak up and that he doesn't have to carry everything alone.

Why it matters to me as a future therapist

I want to work with immigrant families who are trying to balance culture, faith, and communication. I know my story is only one version of this, and plenty of Vietnamese American families look different from mine, so I'll need to ask before I assume.

Try this yourself: think about how love was shown in your house when you were little. Who showed it, and how? Then ask whether that was the only way it could have been shown.

I'm still figuring out what healing looks like, but I think it starts with being able to face where you come from without letting it decide everything about you.
