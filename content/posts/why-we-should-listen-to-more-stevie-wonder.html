The first Stevie Wonder song I remember really hearing was “Sir Duke.” I didn’t know anything about him yet. I just noticed that the song made me feel lighter, and I wanted to play it again.

Lately I’ve been going back to his music a lot, and I’ve started to think it’s worth more than a nostalgia playlist. Here’s why I think more of us should be listening to songs like his.

The music is happy without being shallow

A lot of what I listen to falls into two camps. Either it’s upbeat and doesn’t say much, or it says something important and leaves me feeling heavy. Stevie’s songs manage both at once. “Sir Duke,” the song that first hooked me, is pure joy, a tribute to Duke Ellington and the musicians who came before him. “I Wish” is about childhood memories set to a groove you can’t sit still through.

At the same time, he wrote “Living for the City,” a song about a Black family from Mississippi facing poverty and racism in New York. It still has a beat that pulls you in. I think that’s part of why the message lands. He gets you listening first, then he tells you the truth.

This summer I took notes on a couples therapy training about something called the positive cycle. One line I wrote down was “absence of conflict does not always mean presence of connection.” The trainer said to celebrate good moments first, then slow down and look at what made them work. Stevie’s happiest songs feel like that to me. They notice joy on purpose.

He used his voice to change something real

I didn’t know until recently that Stevie Wonder was one of the biggest champions of making Martin Luther King Jr.’s birthday a national holiday. In 1980 he released “Happy Birthday” and spent years rallying and lobbying for it. President Reagan signed the holiday into law in 1983.

That reminded me that music can be part of how a community pushes for change. As someone who wants to work with families and communities, I find that encouraging. You don’t have to choose between making people feel good and standing for something.

His story is about what’s possible

Stevie lost his sight shortly after he was born. He signed with Motown at 11 and had a number one hit as a teenager. Between 1973 and 1976 his albums Innervisions, Fulfillingness’ First Finale and Songs in the Key of Life won the Grammy for Album of the Year, three releases in a row. No other artist has done that.

I want to be careful here. I don’t think his blindness is the “inspiring” part of his story, and I don’t want to treat it that way. What stays with me is how much he trusted his own ear and kept experimenting, with synthesizers, funk, jazz and gospel, at a time when people expected him to stay in one lane.

I’ve caught this habit in myself before. In one of my classes I wrote that I sometimes slip into seeing people as problems to fix, and that I want to see them “with full humanity and worth.” That applies to how we talk about artists too.

Why it matters to me

I grew up in Philadelphia in a Vietnamese American family. There was love, but it showed through actions, not conversations. My mom worked full-time, cooked and cleaned, and my dad quietly helped around the house in his own way. After I moved away, Vietnamese food became a reminder of love and connection.

Stevie sings out loud the kind of love my family mostly showed quietly, in songs like “Isn’t She Lovely” and “You Are the Sunshine of My Life.” I once wrote that change sometimes “starts quietly, in small moments of connection.” Music can be one of those moments.

Being a guest in a musical tradition that isn’t mine makes me want to listen with respect. Stevie’s music came out of Black church, Motown and the civil rights era. Learning that history makes the songs mean more to me, and I think it’s a good habit for anyone who loves music from a culture they didn’t grow up in.

Music and how we feel

I’m a student, not a therapist yet, so I won’t claim music fixes anything. What I’ve noticed in my own life is that some songs help me feel connected and hopeful on hard days. Stevie’s songs do that for me because they don’t pretend everything is fine. “Love’s in Need of Love Today” admits the world is hurting and still asks us to care for each other.

When I went to therapy, the most healing part for me was the unconditional positive regard my therapist gave me. She trusted me and reassured me that the decisions I was making were best for me. That kind of support gave me confidence in my own goals and dreams. Being heard without judgment, in my family and friendships too, has given me strength and helped me feel more grounded.

Some of Stevie’s songs give me a small piece of that feeling, like someone is in your corner without conditions.

If you’re going through something heavy, music can be good company, and it’s also okay to reach out to a real person. In the U.S. you can call or text 988 any time.

Where to start

  • “Superstition” (Talking Book, 1972)
  • “Higher Ground” and “Living for the City” (Innervisions, 1973)
  • “Sir Duke,” “I Wish” and “Love’s in Need of Love Today” (Songs in the Key of Life, 1976)
  • “Happy Birthday” (Hotter Than July, 1980)

If you like those, try songs from his era with the same mix of groove and message: Sam Cooke’s “A Change Is Gonna Come,” Marvin Gaye’s “What’s Going On,” Curtis Mayfield’s “Move On Up” and Donny Hathaway’s “Someday We’ll All Be Free.”

I want to keep digging into the artists who influenced him and the ones he influenced. If you have a Stevie song that means something to you, I’d like to hear which one and why.

I’m a student sharing what I’m learning. This isn’t therapy or clinical advice.
