Note before we begin: this post talks about mental health care and mentions suicide. No methods are described. If you or someone near you is in crisis, call or text 988 (the Suicide and Crisis Lifeline) in the US, or call 911 for an emergency.

For about ten years we have been told to talk about our mental health. Schools say it. Brands say it. Everybody says it. So people try. They finally say the words, and then they hit a wall.

I’m a grad student training to be a family therapist, so I can see the other side of that wall too. Good clinicians are full. Waitlists are long. That is not laziness. It is math.

The numbers

Here is what the government’s own data says.

  • About 137 million people in the US, roughly 40 percent of the country, live in an area the federal government labels a Mental Health Professional Shortage Area (HRSA, State of the Behavioral Health Workforce, 2025).
  • In a 2025 report, the HHS Inspector General found that 45 percent of surveyed behavioral health providers were not available to take a new patient on Medicare or Medicaid. Of those, 73 percent said they could not take any new patients at all, and many pointed to full caseloads. Among providers who were available, about one in four had a wait of more than 30 days (HHS OIG, report OEI-09-21-00410).
  • In 2024, Asian American adults were 45 percent less likely than US adults overall to have received mental health treatment in the past year. Suicide was the leading cause of death for Asian Americans ages 15 to 24 in 2022 (HHS Office of Minority Health).

That last one matters to me. I’m Vietnamese American. In my house, feelings usually got brushed off instead of talked about, and I know I’m not the only one who grew up that way. I don’t say that to blame my parents. They were working hard and getting through the day. I say it because silence at home and a shortage of providers stack on top of each other. A person who finally gets brave enough to ask can still find nobody to ask.

A note on the numbers. Asian Americans overall die by suicide at a lower rate than the US average, and Asian American adults were 27 percent less likely to report a mental illness in 2024. The treatment gap is still real, and so is the youth number. Both can be true.

Why it happened

I don’t have one clean answer, and I don’t think anyone does. Reports I have read point to low pay for many counseling jobs, long training, burnout, and rural areas with few or no providers. Many providers also do not take insurance, and many insurance networks list providers who never see a single patient. In the 10 counties the OIG studied, an average of 55 percent of the behavioral health providers listed in Medicare Advantage plans did not provide a single service to enrollees in 2023 (HHS OIG, OEI-02-23-00540).

Add stigma on top, and the people who most need the door are the ones least able to find it.

What the gap costs

When someone gets brave once and fails, they may not try twice. I’m a student and I can’t speak for everyone, so I’ll say only this. Quietly giving up looks like a likely ending to a hard search for help, and it never shows up in a statistic about people who got treated.

What I think we should do

Talking was step one. Step two is building the door people walk through. Here is a small version of that, and it takes about ten minutes this week.

  1. Ask one person. Text someone you care about and ask when they last tried to find help, and what happened. Then listen. Don’t fix.
  2. Ask your local government. Find your state or county mental health agency and ask what is being done about provider shortages where you live. Send your state representative the same question. Short and polite works.
  3. Be the logistics. Drive someone, watch their kids, or make the follow-up call with them. Access is mostly logistics, and logistics can be changed.
  4. Save 988. Put it in your phone and tell one other person you did. You can call or text.

I’m still learning how to do this well, and I want to keep reading the data and listening to how people actually try to find help.

I’m a student sharing what I’m learning. This isn’t therapy or clinical advice.
