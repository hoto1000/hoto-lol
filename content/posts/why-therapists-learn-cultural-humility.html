Something I learned in therapy school that changed how I see the world. Part four. This one’s a little longer, because the history matters.

A history of mistrust

For a long time, American medicine and mental health care were built by and for a narrow group of people. From 1932 to 1972, the U.S. Public Health Service ran the Tuskegee study. Hundreds of Black men with syphilis were watched and left untreated, even after penicillin became the standard cure.

Stories like that are part of why many communities still have good reasons to be careful with doctors and therapists.

The first fix

By the late 1980s, people in the field were pushing for change. In 1989, Terry Cross and his colleagues wrote about cultural competence. The idea was that care systems should learn to work well with people from every culture.

It was a real step forward. In practice, though, it sometimes turned into a checklist. Learn five facts about a culture, and you’re competent.

Two doctors in Oakland

In 1998, Melanie Tervalon and Jann Murray-García, who trained doctors at Children’s Hospital Oakland, offered a different word. Humility.

They described cultural humility as a lifelong practice. You keep checking your own assumptions. You notice the power you hold in the room. And you build real partnerships with the communities you serve.

The shift looks small on paper. You stop treating culture as something you can finish learning.

Outside the therapy room

When I learned this, I noticed how often I act like I’ve finished learning about other people. I read one article, or I know one person, and I decide I get it. Cultural humility asks for something slower. It asks me to stay curious.

I’m still working on this. Here are three things I’m trying.

One. When I meet someone from a culture I don’t know well, I ask before I assume.

Two. When I’m the one with more power in a room, like the degree or the title, I try to listen more than I talk.

Three. I learn a community’s history from people in it, through their own writers and organizers.
