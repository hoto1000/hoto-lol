A name for the habit

Someone cuts you off in traffic, and your first thought is “what a jerk.” When you cut someone off, it’s because you’re running late. Psychologists have a name for that.

I’m a therapy grad student, and I’m still learning. So take this as me thinking out loud.

In 1977, the psychologist Lee Ross called it the fundamental attribution error. We tend to explain other people’s actions by who they are, and we forget the situation they’re in.

What the studies found

You can see it in a study from 1967. People read essays praising Fidel Castro. Even when they were told a coin toss decided what the writer had to argue, they still guessed the writer liked Castro.

In 1973, researchers at Princeton asked seminary students to walk to another building and give a talk. Some of them were about to talk about the Good Samaritan. On the way, they passed a man slumped in a doorway. When they weren’t in a hurry, 63 percent stopped to help.

When they were told they were late, only 10 percent did. Their talk topic barely made a difference. The hurry did.

Some of this is learned. In 1984, Joan Miller compared Americans and Hindus in India. The older people got, the more Americans explained behavior by personality, and the more Indians pointed to the situation.

Why it matters

This matters because when we only see the person, we blame them. Someone’s late to work. Someone can’t pay rent.

A kid acts out in class. In therapy school, we learn to ask what’s happening around a person before we ask what’s wrong with them.

It frustrates me how fast my brain jumps to blame. But I have hope, because it’s a habit, and habits can change. I see people do it every day, like the coworker who asks if you’re okay before getting mad, or the teacher who asks what’s going on at home.

Looking at the situation first

Here are three things I’m trying.

One. When someone annoys me, I think of one situation that could explain it before I decide who they are.

Two. Ask “what’s going on?” before “what’s wrong with you?”

Three. Give other people the same grace I give myself on a bad day.
