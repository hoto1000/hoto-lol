A reading of two school debates through the work of Michel Foucault. The ideas below are my own reading of his book Discipline and Punish. I am not speaking for him.

Two education threads caught my attention this week. One asked whether behavior should count toward a grade. The other asked how schools should respond to AI glasses used to cheat on exams in South Korea. They look unrelated, yet both turn on the same question: what are we measuring, and who bears the cost of measuring it?

The behavior score

A behavior score converts conduct into a number, and that number travels with the student. Someone has to define what good behavior looks like. One commenter noted that such scores can disadvantage boys and autistic girls, and I think that deserves attention. A definition of "well behaved" tends to favor the students who already fit it.

A second problem is practical. When a grade blends mastery with compliance, a reader cannot tell what a 90 means. Separating the two at least makes clear what each number measures.

The exam

In Discipline and Punish, Foucault describes the examination as a tool that observes people and ranks them in the same act. The glasses case shows how far that observation has to stretch once the technology becomes small enough to hide. Replies in the thread showed the cost. Oral checks and in-class work take teacher time. Device inspections take proctor time.

I do not think there is a clean answer. A more useful question may be which parts of an exam truly require close watching, and which could become a conversation about the student's thinking.

Why I am writing about this

Foucault asked how institutions decide what counts as normal, and what that decision costs the people inside them. That question applies well beyond school. I plan to keep asking it here, in plain language, and to say clearly when I am unsure.
