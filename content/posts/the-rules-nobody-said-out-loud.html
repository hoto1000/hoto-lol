What does my family do when someone is upset?

Every family has a rulebook. Nobody wrote it down, and nobody remembers signing it. You just know it, the same way you know which stair creaks.

Here is a quick test. Finish this sentence the way your house would: “In our family, when someone is upset, we...”

Notice how fast the answer came. That speed is the rulebook.

Mine

In my house, love looked like food on the table and long work hours. My mom became a pharmacist, so a lot of her care showed up as effort. My dad was quiet and disciplined. Nobody ever sat me down and said “we don’t talk about feelings here.” Feelings just got brushed off, and I learned the rule by watching what happened when someone brought one up.

I didn’t think of any of this as a pattern. It was just how families worked, as far as I knew. That’s the thing about unspoken rules. They feel like gravity.

Why they exist

I’ve stopped treating these rules as flaws. They usually made sense once. When a family is working hard to build a new life, there isn’t a lot of room for processing every feeling out loud. Work gets done. Food gets made. Kids get through school. The rule “keep it together” kept people afloat.

My grandfather did construction work. I doubt anyone in that world had time to sit around naming emotions, and I don’t think they were wrong to prioritize getting through the day.

The trouble starts when the rule outlives the situation that created it. A rule that protected you at twenty can quietly limit you at forty.

In an article first published online on January 14, 2020, researchers studied 322 two-parent families with children ages 13 to 17. They found that facilitative family process rules were associated with more prosocial and less antisocial adolescent communication, while constraining rules were associated with more antisocial communication. The study was published in The Family Journal.

The findings leave room for another possibility: keeping some feelings private can protect privacy or stability in a particular setting. The study focused on two-parent families with adolescents, so it cannot represent every household or settle what a specific rule means.

Where I got it wrong

In training, a role-play client described a family almost identical to mine. My first instinct was to hand her the advice I wish I’d gotten. I had to stop and remind myself that her story isn’t mine, even when it sounds like it.

That was a lesson in how deep these rules run. I thought I was being helpful. I was actually running my own family’s rulebook on her.

Three ways to find your own

  • Watch what gets skipped. What topics does your family step around? Money, health, grief, anger? The gaps show you the rules.
  • Notice what gets praised. Being tough, being quiet, being the smart one, being no trouble. Praise teaches rules faster than lectures do.
  • Check your reflexes. When you’re stressed, what do you do first? Go silent, fix everything, joke, work harder? Odds are you learned that somewhere.

What to do with this

You don’t have to throw the rulebook out. Some of it is good. I’d keep the part about showing love through effort. I’m still deciding what to do about the part where feelings get brushed off.

Culture and family are the reason many of these rules exist, and they deserve respect. They can also be examined. Both can be true.

What I still don’t know

  • Which parts of this rulebook still express care in my family?
  • How do I tell when a familiar reflex is useful and when it needs a closer look?
