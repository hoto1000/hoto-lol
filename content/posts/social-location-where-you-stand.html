You were not born looking at the world from nowhere. Where you stand changes what you see, miss, and assume is "normal."

That spot has a name in my field: social location.

What it actually is

Social location is your mix of race, class, gender, age, religion, language, ability, immigration story, and where you grew up. No one has only one. The parts stack, shaping how people treat you, what doors open, and what you never had to think about.

Think of stadium seats: the front row sees sweat; behind a pole, half the field. Both people may say "I watched the whole game." Only one is right, usually not the person in the good seat.

My seat

I'm Vietnamese American. My grandfather did construction work; my mom became a pharmacist. In my house, love looked like food on the table and long work hours, and feelings were usually brushed off instead of talked about.

During training, a role-play client described almost the same family. I wanted to give her the advice I wish somebody had given me, then stopped myself. Her story isn't mine. My seat is not her seat, even when the view looks close.

That was when social location stopped being a vocabulary word for me.

The part people skip

Everyone loves the idea until it points at them. Talking about others' seats is easy; looking at your own is where people get quiet.

So, plainly:

  • If something has always been easy for you, check why before you call it talent.
  • If something has always been hard for you, check why before you call it your fault.
  • If you've never had to explain a part of yourself to a room, that is information about your seat, not about the room.

None of this means one group is good and another is bad. You and I have different data, and pretending we don't is how people talk past each other for years.

NCES reports that in 2021, computer-based home internet access was available to 98 percent of children ages 3 to 18 in families in the highest income quarter, compared with 85 percent in the lowest. The indicator was updated in August 2023 using American Community Survey data. This is a group estimate, not an explanation of every family's situation.

The strongest caution is that categories can become shortcuts: group patterns do not tell me an individual's story, and overlapping identities can be flattened when the framework is applied mechanically.

Why it matters for helping people

I'm training to be a therapist, so I care about this. If I treat every client like a copy of me, I'll miss most of what they tell me. A behavior can look like a problem from my seat and look like survival from theirs. Culture is usually the reason, and I can't see the reason if I never ask.

Sharing a label doesn't mean I understand a client. Two Asian American people can come from totally different homes. In training, we leaned toward shared assumptions fast, and some were wrong.

A quick exercise

Grab a piece of paper and answer these. No one is grading it.

  1. Where did I grow up, and who else lived there?
  2. What did my family teach me about money, school, and feelings?
  3. What do I get treated as "default" in? What do I get treated as "different" in?
  4. Who would see my life very differently, and why?

Question four is the hard one. Sit with it longer than feels comfortable.

What I still don't know

Which parts of a person's context are useful to ask about, and which am I assuming too quickly? How can I keep a pattern in mind while letting the person's own account lead?

Where I'm going with this

I want to ask before assuming. I'm still working on it and will probably mess it up again. I'd rather catch myself in the mirror than have a client catch me in the room.

I want to notice when I say "that's just how things are," try adding "from where I'm standing," and ask what I still need to learn.
