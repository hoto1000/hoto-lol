The question under the fight

When someone you love yells about the dishes, they might be asking a totally different question. Therapy school taught me what that question is.

I’m a therapy grad student, and I’m still learning. So take this as me thinking out loud.

In 1985, Dr. Sue Johnson and Dr. Les Greenberg first tested something called Emotionally Focused Therapy, or EFT. Johnson centered it on attachment theory, the idea that we all need to feel safe with the people closest to us.

Johnson saw a lot of fights as a kind of protest. Underneath the fight about dishes or money, someone is asking, “Are you there for me? Do I matter to you?”

The cycle couples get stuck in

When that question doesn’t get answered, couples fall into a pattern. One person pushes and criticizes. The other shuts down to keep the peace.

The more one pushes, the more the other pulls away. EFT calls this the negative cycle. It treats the cycle as the enemy, instead of either person.

How EFT helps

In EFT, therapists help people find what’s under the anger. Often it’s hurt, fear, shame or longing.

EFT works in three stages. First, slow the cycle down and name it together. Second, share those softer feelings and learn to answer each other. Third, practice the new way until it feels normal.

Early studies found about 7 in 10 couples were no longer in distress a few months after EFT. Those studies were small, and some researchers have questioned them, so I hold that loosely.

The same loop everywhere

What stays with me is how much this fits the rest of life. When someone sounds angry at work, in our families or online, we react to the loudest part. We argue back or we tune them out.

Then they get louder, and we pull away more. It’s the same cycle, just bigger.

It frustrates me how fast we write people off as “too much.” But I have hope, because I see people slow down and get curious about each other every day.

Getting out of the loop

Here are three things I’m trying.

One. When someone snaps at me, I ask myself what they might be hurt or scared about.

Two. When I’m angry, I try to say the softer feeling underneath, like “I felt left out.”

Three. When a fight keeps repeating, I name the pattern instead of blaming the person. Something like, “We’re in that loop again.”
