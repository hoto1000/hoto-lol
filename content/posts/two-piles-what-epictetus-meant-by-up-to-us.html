A student reading of Epictetus, the Stoic teacher, and his split between what is up to us and what is not. I’m not a classicist. This is a thinking tool I use, and it isn’t a treatment for anything.

Some days the list is too long. Messages to answer, a paper due, a family member who needs something, a news story that sits in my chest. On those days everything feels like it’s mine to fix, and so nothing gets fixed.

A Greek philosopher who lived about nineteen hundred years ago opened his handbook with one sentence that I keep coming back to on days like that. This post is about that sentence, what it can do, and where I think it stops working.

Who Epictetus was

Epictetus was born around 50 CE in Hierapolis, in what is now Turkey. He was born into slavery, and as a young man he was enslaved in Rome, in the household of Epaphroditus, a powerful freedman at the emperor's court. He studied Stoic philosophy while he was still enslaved, and he was freed later. When the emperor Domitian expelled philosophers from Rome, Epictetus moved to Nicopolis in Greece and taught there until he died.

He did not write books. A student named Arrian took notes on his teaching and later published them, along with a short summary called the Enchiridion, which means "handbook." It dates to around 125 CE.

I think his life matters for reading him. He had a great deal taken from him. He knew from the inside how little of a life can be up to the person living it.

The two piles

The handbook starts like this, in Elizabeth Carter's 1758 translation: "Some things are in our control and others not." In our control, he says, are our opinions, our choices, what we want and what we avoid. Our own doing. Not in our control are our bodies, our property, our reputation, and our position. Things that other people and the world decide.

That’s the whole tool. Two piles. Up to me, and not up to me.

His claim is that much of our distress comes from mixing the piles up. We pour energy into the second pile, which we can’t move, and neglect the first, which we can. A later line in the handbook says it more sharply: people are disturbed not by things, but by the views they take of things.

How I use it

When a day feels like too much, I write the list down and sort it. "Whether my professor likes my paper" goes in the second pile. "Whether I spend an hour on the paper tonight" goes in the first. "Whether my family understands my choices" is second pile. "Whether I explain myself kindly one more time" is first pile.

The sorting doesn’t make the second pile disappear. It still matters to me. What changes is where my effort goes. I stop rehearsing conversations I can’t control and spend that time on the one thing I can do.

I also notice that the first pile is almost always smaller than I fear, and more specific. That’s part of why it helps.

The line into therapy

This idea didn’t stay in ancient Greece. Albert Ellis, who developed rational emotive behavior therapy in the 1950s, quoted Epictetus often, especially the line about being disturbed by our views of things. Aaron Beck and his colleagues, in their 1979 book on cognitive therapy for depression, wrote that the philosophical roots of their approach could be traced back to the Stoics.

I want to be careful here. A modern therapy being influenced by an ancient idea doesn’t mean the ancient idea works as therapy. Reading Epictetus isn’t cognitive therapy, and sorting a list isn’t treatment. The connection is a piece of history. It isn’t a prescription.

Where it stops working

The strongest objection to the two piles is one I feel myself. "Focus on what you can control" can be turned into "accept what is done to you." If your landlord won’t fix the heat, or a policy shuts your family out, or someone is hurting you, hearing "that isn’t up to you, let it go" just keeps things as they are.

Epictetus himself has been criticized on this point. He taught people to find freedom inside their own judgments, and he did not, as far as we know, argue against slavery as an institution. A philosophy built for surviving a situation can look very different from one built for changing it.

There is a second problem, and it connects to what I have written about social location. The line between the piles sits in a different place for different people. Money, documents, language, health and who believes you all move things from one pile to the other. Something that’s up to me might not be up to someone who stands somewhere else. The tool works best when I remember that my piles are mine. They aren’t a rule for anyone else.

So I hold it this way. The first pile includes speaking up, organizing, asking for help, and joining other people. Collective action is a choice too. I use the two piles to aim my effort, and I try never to use them as a reason to stop.

What I still don't know

How do you tell the difference between letting go of something that really is out of your hands and giving up on something you could change with help? When a client says "there is nothing I can do," how would a therapist honor that without either agreeing too fast or arguing?

One step

Today, I want to write down three things that are weighing on me and sort them into two piles. Then I want to do one small thing from the first pile, and notice whether the second pile feels any different afterward.
