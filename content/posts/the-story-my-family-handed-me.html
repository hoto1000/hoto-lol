This is the script for one of my TikTok videos. Most of the words come from my own writing and messages. AI helped me organize them and wrote a few connecting lines, and the video uses an AI voice (ElevenLabs) reading them.

Pennsylvania

No one ever invited my parents over for dinner.

I grew up in a white suburban town in Pennsylvania, and it was isolating in many ways. No one made an effort to connect with my parents. That sense of social exclusion pushed them to stay within their Vietnamese circle.

My family came to the United States with the immigrant mindset of survival and sacrifice. My grandparents had to survive. My mom and dad had to survive. They taught us to keep our heads down and not complain.

That made me independent, but also emotionally guarded. Emotions were seen as a distraction from perseverance. There was love, but it showed through actions, not conversations.

My parents took us to parks, and we enjoyed the simple things in life.

I’ve complained about the prejudices put on me in elementary school. But when I look at my mom’s life, the racism I’ve faced is not close to what she experienced. Her motto was, “If there’s a will, there’s a way.”

Leaving

It wasn’t until I went to college and was exposed to more diversity that I began to question some of what I grew up with.

Vietnamese food became a reminder of love and connection, especially after I moved away from Philadelphia.

San Diego

Then I moved to San Diego. Meeting other Asian Americans out here was a culture shock. Things moved so much faster in California. It felt like I’d landed somewhere completely new.

Living in San Diego now, I feel more Americanized, but I still find myself pulled toward my Vietnamese roots. Living between those cultures has often left me feeling pulled in both directions.

Choosing graduate school, knowing my family had financial needs, brought both pride and guilt. I sometimes catch myself worrying about disappointing professors or supervisors, the way I used to worry about disappointing my parents.

The moment I noticed

Then something happened in my Marriage and Family Therapy training. In a role-play, I almost handed a client the advice I wished someone had given me. Her family sounded like mine. I stopped myself, but only just.

That’s when I saw it. The old story was still running. A lot of us learned to carry everything alone, and it follows you.

What’s helping me

So what’s helping me? Three things.

One. I gave the old story a name. In narrative therapy, naming a story helps you see it as something you learned. Mine is “Keep Your Head Down.”

Two. I’m learning our emotional and cultural language, and the social differences around it. In my family, love was in the food. And I’m learning how to respect their values. Parenting isn’t easy, and it was their first time too.

Three. I’m learning to say it out loud. I still work on asking for help and trusting others with my emotions.

Back at the table

I have the privilege to reflect. I think resilience is inherited, but it has to be redefined with each generation.

Healing, to me, means being able to face where you come from without letting it define you.

If I could talk to my younger self, I’d tell him it’s okay to speak up and not carry everything alone. Maybe that starts at dinner.
