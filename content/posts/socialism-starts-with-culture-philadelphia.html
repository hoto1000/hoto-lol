What I learned

Before a country votes for something like socialism, I think neighbors have to practice it first. And I want to start with the city I grew up in, Philadelphia.

As I understand it, socialism means the people who do the work and live with the results get a real say over the things that shape their lives, like housing, work, and care. I know the word scares a lot of people, including many Vietnamese families who left after 1975. I’m still learning, and I could be wrong about parts of this.

In the 1930s, writing from a prison cell, Antonio Gramsci asked why working people didn’t push for change even when they were struggling. Part of his answer was culture. The ideas that feel like common sense, like “everyone for themselves,” hold a system in place as strongly as any law.

Gramsci called the slow work of changing that common sense a war of position. It happens in schools, churches, families, and on the block, long before it shows up in an election.

Philly has been practicing this for a long time. In 1731, Ben Franklin and his friends started a lending library so people could share books none of them could afford alone. In 1736, he helped organize a volunteer fire company. Today you can see it in Weavers Way, a member-owned grocery co-op started in 1973, and in the block captains who organize cleanups and block parties.

We just don’t always give it a name.

Three things I’m trying

I’m still figuring out what this looks like in my own life. Here are three things I’m trying.

One. Use what we already share, like the library, the rec center, and the park, and show up when they’re on the city budget.

Two. When I can, buy from places owned by the people who work or shop there, like a co-op.

Three. Know my block. Learn my neighbors’ names, and ask what the block needs before I decide what it needs.

I want to practice these where I’m from first, and see what grows.
