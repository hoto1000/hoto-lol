What my family carried

My parents never had a word for it. They just kept going.

After 1975, many Vietnamese families left everything behind. Almost 800,000 people who escaped by boat reached other countries. Many others didn’t make it.

My family came to the United States with the immigrant mindset of survival and sacrifice. My grandparents had to survive. My mom and dad had to survive.

How it gets passed down

Here’s something I’ve been learning in school. When a family lives through that much, the pain doesn’t always get talked about. It can still get passed down. It shows up in the rules of the house.

In my house, the rules were simple. Keep your head down. Don’t complain.

Emotions were seen as a distraction from perseverance. There was love, but it showed through actions, not conversations.

Those rules helped my family survive. I understand that now. They also made me independent, and emotionally guarded. I still catch myself worrying about disappointing professors, the way I used to worry about disappointing my parents.

I’m not the only one

I don’t think I’m the only one. The U.S. Office of Minority Health says Asian Americans are 60% less likely than white Americans to get mental health services. A lot of us were taught to handle it alone.

Learning to talk about it

So here are three things I’m trying, if you want to try them with me.

One. I’m giving it a name. When I call it intergenerational trauma, I can see a pattern that started long before me.

Two. I’m asking my family small questions about their lives, and then I just listen. Parenting isn’t easy, and it was their first time too.

Three. I’m getting support. Some therapists speak Vietnamese, and some offer sliding scale fees. If it ever feels like too much, you can call or text 988.

My mom had to survive. I have the privilege to reflect. I think resilience is inherited, but it has to be redefined with each generation.

I want to be part of the generation that talks about it.
