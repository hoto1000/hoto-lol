What should an ethics code ask a therapist to notice about culture and power? Every therapist I'll learn from follows a code of ethics. My program uses the AAMFT Code of Ethics (2015) and the CAMFT Code of Ethics (2020). I read both through a multicultural lens for class. They do a lot right. I still have questions about what they leave out.

What the codes get right

Both ask therapists to respect clients and treat them fairly. AAMFT names diversity and inclusion as part of competent practice, prohibits discrimination, and calls for extra education or supervision when therapists work beyond their experience. Its social-responsibility language reaches beyond therapy.

CAMFT asks therapists to notice how their values and experiences shape their work, and treats continuing education and self-awareness as part of the job. Side by side, I saw both groups building cultural awareness into their rules.

Where I think they stop short

AAMFT talks about culture mostly through competence. That can make it sound like something a therapist masters and checks off. I don't think it works that way. I'm Vietnamese American, and my family still teaches me new things about my own culture.

The versions I read say little about power, privilege, or how unfair systems shape mental health and therapy. CAMFT mentions diversity, but I found its language broad and no direct discussion of intersectionality. That gap can make a "neutral" or color-blind stance easier, brushing past a client's lived experience.

I need to date that criticism. CAMFT's currently posted Code of Ethics says revised standards took effect December 6, 2025. My comments refer to the earlier CAMFT code I read for class. I should compare the current text before describing CAMFT's standards today.

Why it matters in practice

The codes set a solid floor of dignity and fairness. Beyond that, much depends on each therapist's judgment and self-awareness.

A code cannot spell out every context or family meaning. A detailed list might turn cultural humility into boxes to check, so there is a real reason to leave room for judgment. I can see that. I still wonder how clients can tell whether that judgment includes curiosity about power and history.

Reflection may help therapists who already examine their assumptions, but it may not reach someone who has not started. I hadn't thought about it this way until this assignment: a code relying on personal reflection can protect clients only as far as that reflection goes.

A bigger frame

Both codes seem shaped by a mostly Western, individual-focused way of thinking. They explain the expectation to respect differences, yet say less about the systems clients face before therapy. Culture also affects trust, communication, and what healing means. I'd like to see more of that spelled out.

Where I'm going with this

I'm a student and may be missing parts of the picture. I want to keep asking where rules stop and my responsibility starts. That means reflecting on my own culture and assumptions, and asking clients about theirs without guessing.

What I still don't know

How do therapists and supervisors notice when culture shapes a clinical decision? What accountability is possible when a client and therapist read culture or power differently?
