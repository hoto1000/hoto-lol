Verdict: war's sales pitch deserves a hard look. When a country has a large army, people start talking about the enemy it needs. I want to know who made that choice, and when the rest of us agreed.

The country being called an enemy usually gets no say in the story. It appears, almost like a rich young gentleman turning up in a neighborhood where families are looking for husbands. One season, nobody talks about him. The next, people at dinner know all his faults, although they could not find his country on a map. He has not spoken for himself. Still, everyone seems to agree he is dangerous and must be stopped.

On pride

War often starts with proud people in power. They demand courage from other people's sons, who take the risks. They fear being seen backing down. Then they call that fear a matter of principle. Their income can keep consequences far from them until later, if those consequences arrive at all.

When a war he backed goes badly, he may call its cause noble and its execution regrettable. That can make the decisions sound like somebody else's, which is fair because somebody else made them. He will be invited to explain it on television for twenty years. The soldiers who carried out his noble cause will be invited to wait on hold.

On prejudice

The proud men have little power by themselves. They need the rest of us to supply prejudice, and we can be generous with it. A war needs people who choose the good side quickly, with little evidence. It needs a public that finds questions tiresome and doubts suspicious. It needs people who repeat a slogan at dinner and feel, for a moment, part of something large.

I'm learning to watch for the pleasure of a quick judgment. It can feel easier to dislike a whole people than to learn one person's name. A first impression shaped by a government's press office can feel like a costume handed to you. You wear it to someone else's party.

On the people who pay

In an old novel, a bad marriage could ruin one family. A bad war ruins families across a town. Its costs reach beyond the drawing room. A young man comes home and cannot sleep. A city becomes a photograph. A mother on the other side was never part of anyone's argument. Proud men do not pay the bill. The public that accepted prejudice mostly does not pay it either. The cost falls on people farthest from the conversation. That helps explain why the conversation is so easy to have.

What I am asking

I'm not asking anyone to love every government on earth. Some deserve the contempt they get, ours included. I'm asking for a pause before taking a side. Who is selling this war? What do they stand to gain? Will they be anywhere near it? Could we hear from one person in the country we've been told to hate, in their own words, before agreeing that their home should burn?

Changing your mind after judging someone too quickly can give a story a happy ending. If enough of us admit we judged too soon, that change could help end a few more wars. I want to practice taking that second look before deciding who deserves my contempt.
