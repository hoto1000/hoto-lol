When a country has a large army, people start talking about the enemy it needs. I want to ask who made that choice, and when the rest of us agreed to it.

The country being called an enemy usually has no say in the story. People discover it, almost like a rich young gentleman appearing in a neighborhood where families are looking for husbands. One season, nobody is talking about him. The next, people at dinner know all his faults, even when they could not find his country on a map. Before he has spoken for himself, everyone seems to agree that he is dangerous and must be stopped.

On pride

War often begins with people in power who are proud. They require courage from other people's sons, who bear the risks. They fear being seen to back down, and they call that fear a matter of principle. Their income can keep the consequences from reaching them until later, if those consequences arrive at all.

When a war he supported goes badly, he may describe the cause as noble and its execution as regrettable. That can make the decisions seem to belong to someone else, which, to be fair, they did. He will be invited to explain it on television for twenty years. The soldiers who carried out his noble cause will be invited to wait on hold.

On prejudice

The proud men could do very little alone. They depend on the rest of us to supply the prejudice, and we can be generous with it. A war needs a public that decides quickly and with little evidence who the good people are. It needs a public that finds questions tiresome and doubts suspicious. It needs a public that repeats a slogan at dinner and feels, for a moment, part of something large.

I'm learning to watch for the pleasure of a quick judgment. It can feel easier to dislike a whole people than to learn one person's name. A first impression shaped by a government's press office can be like a costume handed to you, one you wear to someone else's party.

On the people who pay

In an old novel, a bad marriage could ruin one family. A bad war ruins families across a town. Its costs reach beyond the drawing room: a young man comes home and cannot sleep, a city becomes a photograph, and a mother on the other side was never part of anybody's argument. The proud men do not pay the bill. The public that accepted prejudice mostly does not pay it either. The cost falls on people farthest from the conversation, which helps explain why the conversation is so easy to have.

What I am asking

I am not asking anyone to love every government on earth. Some deserve the contempt they get, ours included. I am asking for a pause before taking a side. Who is selling this war, what do they stand to gain, and will they be anywhere near it? Could we hear from one person in the country we have been told to hate, in their own words, before agreeing that their home should burn?

Changing your mind after judging a person too quickly can be a happy ending in a story. If enough of us admit we judged too soon, that change could help end a few more wars. I want to practice taking that second look before I decide who deserves my contempt.
