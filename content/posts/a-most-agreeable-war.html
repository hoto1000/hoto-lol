How does a war become agreeable to people who may never pay its cost? War's sales pitch deserves a hard look. When a country has a large army, people start naming the enemy it needs. Who made that choice, and when did the rest of us agree?

In a September 2, 2026 overview, Brown University's Costs of War Project estimated that 905,000 to 940,000 people had died from war violence in post-9/11 war zones as of 2023. The estimate includes U.S. soldiers, allied security forces, civilians, and militants. Read the project’s overview and its estimates.

A fair counterargument is that force may be needed to stop an attack or protect people facing imminent harm. Leaders may have to act with incomplete evidence, and refusing to act can also carry serious costs.

The country called an enemy often gets little say in its story. It is like a rich young gentleman appearing in a neighborhood where families seek husbands. One season, no one mentions him. The next, people at dinner recite his faults, though they could not find his country on a map. He has not spoken, yet everyone agrees he is dangerous and must be stopped.

On pride

War often starts with proud leaders who demand courage from other people's sons. They fear looking weak, then call that fear a principle. Their income can keep consequences far away, if they arrive at all.

When a war he backed goes badly, he may call its cause noble and its execution regrettable. The choices then sound like somebody else's, though somebody else made them. He will explain himself on television for twenty years. The soldiers who carried out his noble cause will be invited to wait on hold.

On prejudice

The proud men need the rest of us to supply prejudice. A war needs people who quickly choose the good side with little evidence, and a public that finds questions tiresome and doubts suspicious. A dinner slogan can make us feel part of something large.

I'm learning to watch for the pleasure in quick judgment. Disliking a whole people can feel easier than learning one person's name. A government's press office can hand us a costume for someone else's party.

On the people who pay

In an old novel, a bad marriage can ruin one family; a bad war ruins families across a town. A young man comes home unable to sleep. A city becomes a photograph. A mother on the other side was never part of the argument. Proud men and a prejudiced public rarely pay the bill. The cost falls on people farthest from the conversation, which may explain why it is so easy to have.

What I am asking

I'm not asking anyone to love every government on earth. Some deserve contempt, ours included. I'm asking for a pause before taking a side. Who sells this war, what do they gain, and will they be near it? Could we hear one person from the country we have been told to hate, in their own words, before agreeing that their home should burn?

What I still don't know

What evidence would let the public assess claims before supporting force? How can we hear from people in another country without treating one person as a stand-in for millions? Which checks on executive power work when Congress and the public disagree?

Changing your mind after judging someone too quickly can give a story a happy ending. If more of us admit we judged too soon, that could help end a few more wars. I want to practice taking a second look before deciding who deserves my contempt.
