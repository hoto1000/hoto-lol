Where is a kid supposed to put the anger?

I keep coming back to two groups of kids. Some get pulled into crime. Some live with ghosts: a friend who died, a parent who left or was taken away, a night they keep replaying. Often they're the same kids.

My first answer was simple. Give them somewhere to go. A skate park that stays open at night.

A wall where graffiti is allowed. A breakdance circle, a rap battle, a gym that teaches defense instead of offense. A church, a temple, a cushion to meditate on.

The longer I sat with that list, the more it looked like a question about society. Why do so many kids have nowhere to put what they carry? Who decided that? And what does it say about us that when kids invent their own outlets, we so often treat the outlet as the crime?

I'm training to become a marriage and family therapist. This is a student working through research and philosophy. It isn't a program design, and it isn't advice.

A city with no room for teenagers

Look at where a teenager without money is allowed to be. The mall wants customers. The coffee shop wants a purchase.

The plaza has metal knobs bolted to its ledges so nobody can skate them. The park closes at dusk.

The sociologist Ray Oldenburg wrote about “third places,” the spots outside home and work where people gather and belong. For adults those places mostly cost money. For kids without it, the third place is the street, and the street is where they get told to move along.

The French philosopher Henri Lefebvre called this a question of the “right to the city”: the right to shape a city and use it, along with the right to visit it. The architectural historian Iain Borden argues that skateboarders make that claim with their bodies. They take a handrail built for one purpose and use it for another.

Skating is a small argument about who public space is for. Our usual answer, through skate stoppers, loitering rules, and curfews, is that it isn't for them.

Curfews show how much that answer is about how things look. A 2016 Campbell systematic review by Wilson and colleagues looked at 12 evaluations and concluded that juvenile curfews do not reduce crime or victimization. Crime during curfew hours even rose slightly.

Meanwhile, federal data have long shown that on school days, violent crime by juveniles peaks between 3 and 4 in the afternoon, right after the bell. On non-school days it peaks in the evening (Snyder & Sickmund, 1999). We keep trying to clear kids off the streets at midnight, when the clearer problem is the empty hours we never planned for.

The kids already built this once

Here's what struck me most. My list (graffiti, breakdancing, rap battles, a place to gather at night) isn't random. It's almost exactly the four elements of hip hop: writing, breaking, MCing, and DJing. Kids invented that list themselves, under some of the worst conditions an American city has produced.

In the late 1960s and 1970s, the South Bronx was cut apart by the Cross Bronx Expressway, hollowed out by disinvestment, and burned by waves of fires while services were pulled back. Gangs filled the gap.

Then, in December 1971, a peacemaker named Cornell “Black Benjie” Benjamin was beaten to death while trying to negotiate between gangs. His own gang, the Ghetto Brothers, chose not to retaliate. Their leader called a peace meeting at the Boys Club on Hoe Avenue, and a truce followed.

Historians like Jeff Chang credit that truce with opening the streets and parks for the parties where hip hop grew, including DJ Kool Herc’s 1973 back-to-school party on Sedgwick Avenue.

Think about what that means. Young people facing violence, poverty, and grief turned battles into dance battles, turf into crews, and insults into rhymes. They built the outlet society wouldn’t build for them.

And the city’s response to one of those elements, graffiti, was decades of crackdowns. The same art form now sells in galleries. When the kids do it on a train, it’s vandalism.

When it hangs on a wall downtown, it’s culture. The paint is the same. What changed is who holds the brush and who owns the wall.

Rage as a social signal

Part of my first idea was that kids need to get their rage out. The research complicates that. In a well known experiment, Brad Bushman (2002) had angry people hit a punching bag while thinking about the person who made them angry.

They came out angrier and more aggressive than people who did nothing. Rage doesn’t drain like water from a tank.

I still think rage is also a social signal, beyond a private feeling to be managed.

Robert Merton argued in 1938 that crime grows where a society holds up the same goals for everyone (success, money, respect) while blocking the legitimate paths to them for some people. The sociologist Elijah Anderson, in Code of the Street (1999), described how in neighborhoods where people do not trust the police or the courts to protect them, toughness and the readiness to fight become a kind of protection. Much of the violence he describes is defensive. It’s about avoiding being seen as an easy target.

That reframes my phrase “defense, not offense.” Many kids are already playing defense.

The question is whether society gives them a form of defense that builds them up or one that gets them killed. Martial arts may be one answer. A 2017 meta-analysis by Harwood, Lavidor, and Rassovsky found that 11 of 12 controlled studies showed martial arts training reduced aggression in children and teens, though the authors called the research sparse.

What martial arts seem to teach is restraint, respect, and a teacher who watches. That’s different from blowing off steam.

The strongest evidence I found came from Chicago. Becoming a Man, a school-based program that helps teen boys slow down and think before reacting in heated moments, was tested in two randomized trials. Heller and colleagues (2017) reported that violent-crime arrests fell by 45 to 50 percent during the program.

The program never told boys their instincts were wrong. It helped them notice when an instinct built for a dangerous street was being used in a moment that didn’t need it.

The neighborhood is the program

An outlet alone is not enough. A 2016 meta-analysis by Spruit and colleagues, covering more than 132,000 adolescents, found no overall link between playing sports and lower delinquency.

In a Swedish study, Mahoney and Stattin (2000) found that structured activities were linked to low antisocial behavior, while unstructured hanging out with little adult guidance was linked to more. A skate park at night with nobody around is just a place. It isn’t a bond yet.

The sociologist Travis Hirschi argued in 1969 that people stay out of trouble mostly because of bonds: attachment to people, commitment to goals, involvement in activities, and belief in shared rules.

Robert Sampson and colleagues found something similar at the level of whole neighborhoods. Surveying 8,782 Chicago residents across 343 neighborhoods, they found that “collective efficacy,” trust among neighbors plus their willingness to step in for the common good, was linked to less violence. Poverty and instability seemed to raise violence largely by wearing that trust down (Sampson, Raudenbush, & Earls, 1997).

Even the physical place matters. In a randomized trial in Philadelphia, cleaning and greening vacant lots was followed by a 29 percent drop in gun violence near the lots in the poorest neighborhoods (Branas et al., 2018). Nobody gave a lecture. The block just started to look like someone cared about it.

So the outlet works when it is woven into a web of people. The skate park needs the older skater who teaches the younger one. The cypher needs its rules about respect.

The dojo needs its teacher. The people are what make the place work.

The kids who see ghosts

The sociologist Avery Gordon wrote that haunting is how past social violence makes itself felt in the present, even when official history says it’s over.

I think that is one way to understand kids who see ghosts. They are carrying grief that the wider society has not reckoned with: the friend lost to a shooting, the block that burned, the parent who is gone.

I also wrote the word fatherless in my first notes, and I want to handle it carefully, because many single parents do heroic work and most kids raised without a father never commit a crime. McLanahan, Tach, and Schneider (2013) reviewed the most careful studies and found father absence does seem to harm some outcomes, with the clearest evidence for high school graduation and social and emotional adjustment, though the effects are smaller than simpler studies suggest.

Absence also isn’t only a private family choice. By 2010, one in every 28 children in the United States had a parent behind bars, up from one in 125 about 25 years earlier (Pew Charitable Trusts, 2010). Some of the fatherlessness we blame on families was produced by policy.

Mentoring is one way to add steady adults back. In a large study of Big Brothers Big Sisters, Tierney, Grossman, and Resch (1995) found that young people matched with a mentor were 46 percent less likely to start using drugs and almost a third less likely to hit someone than kids still on the waitlist. A mentor can’t replace a parent. A mentor is one more person who shows up.

Spirit, of any kind

My notes said kids need spirituality, and that it does not matter which God. A 2001 meta-analysis by Baier and Wright reviewed 60 studies and found religious beliefs and practices had a moderate deterrent effect on crime.

I want to be careful with that. Kids who attend services differ in many ways from kids who do not, and nobody should be pushed into a faith.

Emile Durkheim offers a different way to read it. He argued that religion is, among other things, a society gathering and feeling itself as one. He called the shared rush of a crowd moving together “collective effervescence.”

A church service has it. So does a temple full of chanting, a meditation hall in silence, and, I would argue, a cypher when the whole circle reacts to one line. Maybe what kids need from spirituality has less to do with a particular God, and more to do with belonging to something larger than their anger, with people who expect them back next week.

What we choose to pay for

A 2020 report found that the average cost of the most expensive confinement option for a young person was $214,620 per year (Justice Policy Institute, cited in Sticker Shock 2020). I keep holding that number next to the empty skate park and the locked rec center. We already spend money on these kids. We just choose to spend it after the harm instead of before it.

That’s the deepest thing this question taught me. Crime by young people is partly a story about choices. It’s also a story about what a society builds, what it tears down, which spaces it opens and which ones it locks, and which kids it decides are worth investing in early.

The kids in the Bronx proved decades ago that young people will make meaning out of almost nothing. I want to learn how the rest of us can meet them halfway.

What I still don’t know

I couldn’t find solid research on night skate parks or legal graffiti walls and crime. That doesn’t prove they fail. It means nobody has measured them well.

I don’t know which outlets help which kids, or whether a night skate park with one trusted adult would look more like Becoming a Man or more like an empty youth center. I would love to see a city try it and count honestly.
