Last fall, one of my classes had us watch a video called What Is Privilege? The setup was simple. A group of people stood side by side in one straight line. Someone read statements out loud, and people took a step forward or a step back depending on whether each one was true for them.

Everyone started even. By the end, they were spread out all over the place.

Nobody in that line had picked where they ended up. They were just answering questions about lives they were born into.

The statement that got me

Most of the statements were ones I expected, things about money, race, and safety. Then one came up about growing up with both parents in the home.

I had never thought of that as privilege. It was just my normal. But it makes sense. Two parents can mean more stability, more support, more hands and more money. That kind of foundation shapes how a kid grows up, and almost nobody counts it when they talk about who "earned" what.

That was the moment privilege stopped feeling like a word other people needed to think about.

Where I'd be standing

Watching people move forward and backward made me uncomfortable. Knowing inequality exists is one thing. Seeing it laid out in a line of real people made it hard to look away from.

So I tried to figure out where I'd be standing. I'm Vietnamese American. I've had real advantages, like access to education. I've also dealt with cultural stereotypes and the feeling of being out of place in rooms where nobody looked like me.

I'd probably be somewhere in the middle, stepping forward on some questions and back on others. Privilege comes in layers. Most people have some of it and lack some of it, and which layer matters depends on the room you're in.

The effort story

Here's the blunt part. A lot of people believe success only comes down to effort. Work hard and you'll make it. If you didn't make it, you didn't work hard enough.

Effort matters. I believe that. But that story skips over the starting line, and that's where prejudice sneaks in. Once you believe everyone started even, the people behind you start to look lazy. You end up blaming them for barriers they never built and can't always see past.

Why this matters to me

I'm training to be a therapist, and clients are going to come from every kind of background. If I don't think about privilege, I'll miss big parts of their story.

The example I keep in mind is simple. If a client is struggling and my answer is basically "just work harder," without noticing the systems pushing against them, I could do real harm. I'd be asking them to fit my standard when my job is to meet them where they are.

The same goes outside of therapy. It's easy to assume everyone has the same chances as you. It takes a little more effort to stop and ask what someone might be up against.

It's not about guilt

The video also reminded me of something I needed to hear. Privilege isn't about feeling guilty. I didn't choose the circumstances I was born into, and neither did you.

What I do get to choose is how I respond. I can use the advantages I have to understand other people better, question my own assumptions, and show some empathy before I judge.

Try your own line

Grab a piece of paper. For each one that's true for you, take one step forward. Nobody is grading this.

  1. I grew up with both parents in the home.
  2. My family never worried about keeping the lights on.
  3. The language spoken at home matched the language at school.
  4. I saw people who looked like me in my textbooks and on TV.
  5. My family could help me pay for college.
  6. I've never been followed around a store.
  7. I've never had to wonder if a building would be accessible to me.

Now look at how many steps you took. Then think about someone you know who would have taken fewer.

Where I'm going with this

I want to keep building this awareness, because it's easy to lose. Next time I catch myself thinking someone just needs to try harder, I want to ask what their line looked like first.
