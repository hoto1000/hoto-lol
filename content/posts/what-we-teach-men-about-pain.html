A note before we begin: I am doing well. I am writing this piece to draw attention to a problem I see around me, and I am not writing it from a crisis of my own.

What does it cost when boys learn to keep pain to themselves?

We teach men to hide pain, then wonder why they stay quiet. Males are about half the U.S. population and nearly 80 percent of its suicide deaths, according to the CDC (2023 figures). In 2024 the male suicide rate was nearly four times the female rate. I am training to become a marriage and family therapist; those numbers have stayed with me since I first read them.

I grew up as an Asian American boy in a house where hard feelings stayed quiet and private. Nobody announced the rule. Adults passed along what they had been taught, and I absorbed it. Years later, I keep asking what the silence costs and why professional help misses men. This essay asks without blaming men or women.

What the numbers show

Suicide is the most severe outcome in a wider pattern. The CDC reports this sex disparity each year; a federal brief shows the male rate was three to four times the female rate throughout 2014 to 2024.

The National Center for Health Statistics' September 2026 report gives a more concrete view: the 2024 age-adjusted male rate was 22.3 deaths per 100,000 standard population. The report and its tables use final mortality data.

In the National Institute of Mental Health's summary of 2022 survey data, 41.6 percent of men with any mental illness received past-year treatment, compared with 56.9 percent of women. For adults with serious mental illness, the figures were 59.3 percent of men and 71.4 percent of women.

Women are diagnosed with depression and anxiety more often, so their higher treatment rate does not mean they are doing well. The figures suggest care reaches a smaller share of men who need it. The survey was conducted in English and recorded only male and female categories, so it cannot describe every population.

These comparisons cannot explain why treatment use or suicide rates differ. Underlying distress, recognition, reporting, resources, and access may also matter. A men-focused essay can miss the needs of women and people outside the survey's male and female categories. I can take the disparity seriously while staying careful about its causes.

Distress that goes unrecognized

Part of the gap may reflect what clinicians and screening tools notice. In a 2013 JAMA Psychiatry study, Martin and colleagues found depressed men reported more anger attacks, substance use, and risk-taking than depressed women. Standard checklists omit these symptoms. Adding them to the usual symptoms erased the difference in depression rates in their sample. A checklist that misses them is a lousy gatekeeper.

One study cannot settle the question. Distress may go unrecognized in some men. Someone whose depression appears as irritability or heavy drinking can pass a standard screen unidentified.

The rules many men inherit

The American Psychological Association's guidelines for work with boys and men note that traits associated with masculinity, such as courage and leadership, are often linked to good health. Other masculine norms can cause harm. Men taught to suppress emotion are less willing to seek help.

Wong, Ho, Wang, and Miller (2017) pooled 78 samples with more than 19,000 participants. Conformity to masculine norms was modestly associated with poorer mental health and moderately associated with less help-seeking. Self-reliance was among the norms most consistently tied to poor outcomes. Primacy of work showed no significant relationship to any outcome. These are correlations and cannot establish cause.

That result complicates a lazy story about work. Responsibility and providing for others may carry little risk on their own. Managing everything alone appears more closely tied to harm.

One perspective among many

I can speak only for myself. Research on Asian Americans finds lower mental health service use than in the general population, and higher use among U.S.-born Asian Americans than immigrants (Abe-Kim et al., 2007). Family expectations, immigration history, and generation all appear to play a part. Asian American men are a large and varied group, and my experience is one account among many.

Family systems theory helps me see how “handle it quietly” can travel through generations without a villain. Each person may be responding sensibly to the circumstances they faced.

What may help

I do not have one remedy. A few approaches have support or plausible logic. Help may land more readily when framed through values men already hold, such as building strength, solving a problem, or caring for people who depend on them. Families, friends, and workplaces can make it routine to ask a second question after “How are you?” Clinicians can ask about anger, sleep, drinking, and risk-taking along with sadness.

What I still don't know

Whether a practical first conversation would bring more men through the door remains an open question. The answer probably varies from person to person.

How can clinicians ask about anger, sleep, drinking, or risk-taking without deciding too quickly what those signs mean for a particular person? Which approaches help men feel able to seek support while honoring differences in family, culture, and identity?

If you or someone you know is struggling

In the United States, call or text 988 to reach the Suicide and Crisis Lifeline at any hour. If someone is in immediate danger, call 911. This essay is a personal reflection and does not offer medical advice or treatment.
