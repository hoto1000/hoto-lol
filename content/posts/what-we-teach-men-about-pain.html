A note before we begin: I am doing well. I am writing this piece to draw attention to a problem I see around me, and I am not writing it from a crisis of my own.

Verdict: We teach men to hide pain, then wonder why they stay quiet. Males make up about half of the U.S. population and nearly 80 percent of its suicide deaths, according to the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (2023 figures). In 2024 the male suicide rate was nearly four times the female rate. I am training to become a marriage and family therapist, and those numbers have stayed with me since I first read them.

I grew up as an Asian American boy in a house where hard feelings stayed quiet and private. Nobody announced the rule. The adults around me passed along what they had been taught, and I absorbed it the same way. Years later, I keep asking what that silence costs and why professional help misses men. This essay asks that question without blaming men or women.

What the numbers show

Suicide is the most severe outcome in a wider pattern, so the figures need context. The CDC reports the sex disparity every year. A federal data brief covering 2014 to 2024 shows the male rate running between three and four times the female rate throughout the decade.

Treatment shows a similar split. In the National Institute of Mental Health's summary of 2022 survey data, 41.6 percent of men with any mental illness received treatment in the past year, against 56.9 percent of women. Among adults with serious mental illness, 59.3 percent of men were treated, compared with 71.4 percent of women.

Women are diagnosed with depression and anxiety more often than men, so their higher treatment rate does not mean they are doing well. The figures do suggest that care reaches a smaller share of men who need it. The survey also has limits. It was conducted in English and recorded only the categories male and female, so it cannot describe every population.

Distress that goes unrecognized

Part of the gap may come from what clinicians and screening tools are prepared to notice. In a 2013 study in JAMA Psychiatry, Martin and colleagues found that depressed men reported more anger attacks, substance use, and risk-taking than depressed women. Standard depression checklists do not ask about these symptoms. When the researchers counted them alongside the usual symptoms, the difference in depression rates between men and women disappeared in their sample. A checklist that misses those symptoms is a lousy gatekeeper.

One study cannot settle the question. That is a useful warning label. Distress may go unrecognized in some men, and that possibility has a practical consequence. A man whose depression appears as irritability or heavy drinking can pass through a standard screening without being identified.

The rules many men inherit

The American Psychological Association's guidelines for work with boys and men point out that traits associated with masculinity, such as courage and leadership, are often linked to good health. Other masculine norms can cause harm. Men taught to suppress their emotions are less willing to seek help.

Wong, Ho, Wang, and Miller (2017) pooled 78 samples with more than 19,000 participants. Their analysis found that conformity to masculine norms was modestly associated with poorer mental health and moderately associated with less help-seeking. Self-reliance was among the norms most consistently tied to poor outcomes. Primacy of work showed no significant relationship to any outcome. These are correlations, so the analysis cannot establish cause.

That last result cuts through a lazy story about work. Responsibility and providing for others may carry little risk on their own. The pressure to manage everything alone appears to be the part most closely tied to harm.

One perspective among many

I can speak only for myself. Research on Asian Americans and mental health services finds lower service use than in the general population, and higher use among U.S.-born Asian Americans than among immigrants (Abe-Kim et al., 2007). Family expectations, immigration history, and generation all appear to play a part. Asian American men are a large and varied group, and my experience is one account among many.

Family systems theory gives me a way to think about how a rule like “handle it quietly” can travel through generations without a villain. Each person in the chain may be responding sensibly to the circumstances they faced.

What may help

I do not have one remedy to offer. A few approaches have support or plausible logic behind them. Help may land more readily when it is described through values men already hold, such as building strength, solving a problem, or caring for the people who depend on them. Families, friends, and workplaces can make it routine to ask a second question after “How are you?” Clinicians can ask directly about anger, sleep, drinking, and risk-taking, along with sadness.

Whether a practical first conversation would bring more men through the door remains an open question. The answer probably varies from person to person. I want to keep thinking about how that first question is asked.

If you or someone you know is struggling

In the United States, call or text 988 to reach the Suicide and Crisis Lifeline at any hour. If someone is in immediate danger, call 911. This essay is a personal reflection and does not offer medical advice or treatment.
