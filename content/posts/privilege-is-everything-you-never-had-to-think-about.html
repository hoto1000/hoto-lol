Let's get the fight out of the way. When people hear privilege, many hear "you're guilty" and stop listening. That's fair, given how the word gets thrown around. It also helps explain why the conversation goes nowhere.

My plain definition: privilege is the list of things that were easy for you that you never noticed were easy.

The invisible list

You don't feel privilege while you have it. Nobody wakes up thinking, "wow, I sure didn't get followed around the store today." The list becomes visible beside someone else's.

Some examples of things that can be on or off a person's list:

  • Walking into a room and not wondering if you're the only one like you
  • Never having to translate for your parents at the doctor's office
  • Having someone in your family who already went to college and could explain how it works
  • Having your name pronounced correctly on the first try
  • Not worrying that one bad day will be read as proof about your whole group

NCES reports that in 2021, home internet access through a computer was available to 98 percent of children ages 3 to 18 whose parents had a bachelor's degree or higher, compared with 78 percent whose parents had less than a high school credential. The indicator was updated in August 2023. This is a group comparison, not a full account of any child's life.

None of these make anyone bad. They're starting conditions. Some people get a tailwind, others a headwind, and many get both at different points.

Why this is not a scoreboard

I've seen this become a contest over who had it worse. I don't think that helps. Someone can be ahead in one area and behind in another. Social location is a stack, and it looks different for everybody.

The strongest objection is that privilege talk can rank people, discount effort, or sound like blame for hardship they didn't create. That risk is real when the idea replaces curiosity about someone's conditions.

I'm Vietnamese American. My family's story includes sacrifice I'm still figuring out how to repay. It brings real challenges. My mom also worked hard enough to become a pharmacist, and my family valued school. Both are true; neither cancels the other.

What I got wrong

Early on, I assumed that if something was hard for me, it was hard for everyone like me. I treated my own list as the community's list. In training, I wanted to give a role-play client the advice I would have wanted. Her list wasn't mine.

That's the practical danger of not seeing your own privilege: you start giving advice that only works from your seat.

How to start seeing yours

Try this honestly. Nobody's checking:

  1. Write down three things that were easy in your life.
  2. Next to each one, write who helped make it easy.
  3. Write down one thing that was hard, and ask whether it was hard for everyone or just for people in your seat.

If step two gives you a long list of names, good. That's not shameful. That's information.

What I still don't know

How do I notice an advantage without assuming someone else's life is easier? What does accountability look like when I did not create a condition but may still benefit from it?

Where I'm going with this

I want to notice my list before someone else points it out and stop flinching when privilege comes up. The flinch is usually a signal worth looking at. Before I give advice, I want to ask what's easy for me that I've never had to think about, and whose help is underneath it.

Student writing, not clinical advice. If you are struggling, a licensed professional or a local crisis line can help.
