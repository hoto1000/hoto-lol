Let's get the fight out of the way first. When people hear the word privilege, a lot of them hear "you're guilty" and stop listening. That's a fair reaction to how the word gets thrown around, and it's also why the conversation goes nowhere.

So here is my plain definition. Privilege is the list of things that were easy for you that you never noticed were easy.

The invisible list

You don't feel privilege while you have it. That's the whole trick. Nobody wakes up and thinks, "wow, I sure didn't get followed around the store today." You only notice the list when someone else's list is different.

Some examples of things that can be on or off a person's list:

  • Walking into a room and not wondering if you're the only one like you
  • Never having to translate for your parents at the doctor's office
  • Having someone in your family who already went to college and could explain how it works
  • Having your name pronounced correctly on the first try
  • Not worrying that one bad day will be read as proof about your whole group

None of these make anyone a bad person. They're just starting conditions. Some people got a tailwind, some got a headwind, and a lot of people got both on different parts of the road.

Why this is not a scoreboard

I've seen people turn this into a contest over who had it worse. I don't think that helps anyone. Someone can be ahead in one area and behind in another. Social location is a stack, remember, and the stack is different for everybody.

I'm Vietnamese American, and my family's story includes sacrifice that I'm still figuring out how to repay. That comes with real challenges. It also came with things I had, like a mom who worked hard enough to become a pharmacist and a family that valued school. Both are true. I have to hold both without making one cancel the other.

What I got wrong

Early on, I assumed that when something was hard for me, it must be hard for everyone like me. I was treating my own list as the list for a whole community. Looking back, I did that in training too, when I wanted to give a role-play client the advice I would have wanted. Her list wasn't mine.

That's the practical danger of not seeing your own privilege. You start giving advice that only works from your seat.

How to start seeing yours

Try this, and be honest, because nobody's checking:

  1. Write down three things that were easy in your life.
  2. Next to each one, write who helped make it easy.
  3. Write down one thing that was hard, and ask whether it was hard for everyone or just for people in your seat.

If step two gives you a long list of names, good. That's not shameful. That's information.

Where I'm going with this

I want to get better at noticing my list before someone else points it out. I also want to stop flinching when the word comes up, because the flinch is usually the signal that something's worth looking at.

If you take one thing from this, make it a question: what's easy for me that I've never had to think about?
