For one class assignment, I had to make a single roll of toilet paper last a whole week. That's it. No running to the store, no asking a roommate. One roll.

I thought it would be the easiest assignment of the semester. It became an annoying lesson in what scarcity does to your head.

Why it was easy for me, and why that's the point

I live in an apartment with my older brother, who has his own bathroom. I didn't share, so I controlled my use. I shop at Costco, where I can buy in bulk and stretch my money. One roll got me through the week without much trouble.

A mild inconvenience for me could be a real stressor for someone else.

What happened anyway

I started tracking the roll. I folded sheets more carefully, checked what remained, and calculated how long it would last.

Something I never think about became a small source of anxiety. I knew I could buy more once the assignment ended, but it took up more room in my head than I expected.

The part that felt like shame

The article we read, by Laszloffy and Habekost (2010), says this kind of task is meant to simulate daily life for people living in poverty. Scarcity isn't only having less. It brings stress, shame, and a constant need to keep track of what you have.

I never ran out. Imagining asking a classmate for a roll embarrassed me. I saw how vulnerable it can feel to admit you don't have enough, even over something small.

I used to think I was just frugal

I've always thought of myself as frugal. After this week, I realized that for me frugality is a choice: I can plan ahead, buy in bulk, and save because I have the money.

For many people, frugality isn't optional. Money problems pile onto everything else and drain energy that people with more cushion may not notice.

On September 15, 2026, the U.S. Census Bureau reported a 10.2% official poverty rate for 2025. Its measure uses pretax money income and excludes tax credits, so it offers one national measure, not a full account of household scarcity. The Census Bureau's 2025 income and poverty release explains the measure.

Why it matters for the work I want to do

I'm training to be a therapist. If one roll made me uncomfortable for a week, I can imagine how much harder it is to admit you can't provide food or shelter for your family.

Clients need a place to say those things without judgment. I want to respect their courage and resourcefulness. Scarcity can bring creativity, anxiety, irritability, or shame, and I shouldn't assume which I'm seeing.

A fair objection is that this week could not reproduce poverty: I knew it would end and could buy more. Treating temporary discomfort as understanding risks overstating what I learned.

What I still don't know

  • How might this land for someone who has lived with poverty?
  • What does one roll leave out about scarcity?
  • How can I listen without treating one uncomfortable week as knowledge of someone else's life?
