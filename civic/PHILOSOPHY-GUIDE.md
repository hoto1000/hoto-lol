# Philosophical and biblical foundations: a guide for "Where do my political views sit?"

Purpose: use major traditions of political thought, the Bible, and the Roman sources behind them as a **checklist for fairness**. They help make sure each statement pair carries the strongest reason each side would actually give.

How to use this: these sources inform the *reasons* inside statements. The quiz itself should not quote thinkers or scripture. Naming authorities in a question turns a policy choice into a test of loyalty to that authority. It can also make non-religious visitors, or visitors from other faiths, feel the quiz isn't for them.

---

## 1. The big finding: no single tradition maps onto left or right

This is the strongest reason the quiz scores seven areas separately.

- **Catholic social teaching** (from Rome, the Vatican) rejects *both* unrestrained free competition and socialism. In *Quadragesimo Anno* (Pius XI, 1931), unchecked competition is said to destroy itself (para. 109), and the encyclical also says a person can't be both a good Catholic and a true socialist (para. 120). The same tradition supports a just wage *and* private property, and both welcoming migrants *and* letting nations regulate migration.
- **The Bible** is cited by people on both sides of nearly every item in this quiz. Romans 13 is the clearest example (section 3).
- **Moral Foundations Theory**, the main modern research on moral intuitions, finds people weigh several values. Political groups differ in emphasis, not in having different values entirely (section 2).

Design rule that follows: **a religious or philosophical background never predicts a result**. This matches the brief's rule not to imply any group thinks alike.

## 2. Modern moral psychology: Moral Foundations Theory

- Graham, Haidt, and Nosek (2009) found that in their US samples, liberals relied mainly on care and fairness, while conservatives drew on all five foundations then in the theory: care, fairness, loyalty, authority, and purity.
- In 2023 the theory split fairness into **equality** and **proportionality** (getting what you earn). The current list is care, equality, proportionality, loyalty, authority, and purity (Atari et al., 2023). Proportionality is endorsed by most people and slightly more by people on the right. Liberty is a candidate foundation.
- The theory is descriptive, not a claim about which values are right (moralfoundations.org).

**How it guides the quiz: a balance audit.** For each pair, check which foundation each side's reason appeals to.
1. Each side should get a reason its own supporters would find persuasive, not just a reason the other side recognizes.
2. Don't give the right-leaning side only authority or purity reasons and the left-leaning side only care reasons. Both sides use care, fairness, and liberty arguments.
3. If one side has a reason and the other has none, that's an imbalance.

## 3. The Bible, and where it leads back to Rome

Paul's letter to the **Romans** was written to Christians living in the capital of the empire. Its most political passage, **Romans 13:1–7**, has shaped Christian thinking about government ever since.

**Romans 13: one passage, used on both sides**
- In June 2018, Attorney General Jeff Sessions cited Romans 13 to defend strict prosecution of illegal border crossings. The White House press secretary said enforcing the law was "very biblical" (CNN, 2018).
- Other Christians answered with other passages and readings:
  - **Acts 5:29**, "We must obey God rather than men," and Daniel's refusal of a royal decree. The evangelical writer John Stott argued civil disobedience becomes a duty when human law conflicts with God's law.
  - The Southern Baptist Convention passed an immigration-reform resolution citing scripture six times.
  - Some scholars read Romans 13 as local advice to Roman Christians, not a general theory of the state.
- **Quiz areas it touches:** government power (g2), immigration (i2, i3), civil liberties (c3).

**"Render to Caesar" (Mark 12:17)**
- Jesus says to give Caesar what is Caesar's and God what is God's.
- It's read as support for a separate religious and civil sphere, and also as a limit on what the state can claim.
- **Quiz area:** religion in public schools (s3).

**Immigration passages cited on different sides**
- Welcome side: Leviticus 19:33–34 (treat the foreigner as native-born, "for you were foreigners in Egypt"), Matthew 25:35 ("I was a stranger and you welcomed me"), the Good Samaritan (Luke 10).
- Order and law side: Romans 13:1–5.
- Christianity Today's study on this topic notes that scripture doesn't translate directly into public policy.

**Rome as the seat of Catholic teaching**
- **Subsidiarity** (*Quadragesimo Anno*, para. 79–80): it is wrong to take from smaller groups what they can do themselves and hand it to a larger body. The higher authority should handle what only it can do. *Quiz area:* g1 education standards, g3 size of government. Note that subsidiarity is a *test*, not a verdict. It allows national action when local bodies can't do the job.
- **Just wage and private property** (*Rerum Novarum*, Leo XIII, 1891): defends private ownership and rejects socialism. It also holds that wages must be enough to live in reasonable, frugal comfort, supports worker associations, and says the state has a duty to intervene for justice. *Quiz area:* e1 minimum wage, e3 business rules.
- **Migration** (*Catechism of the Catholic Church*, 2241): prosperous nations should welcome, as far as they can, foreigners seeking security and a livelihood. Political authorities may set legal conditions on immigration for the common good, and immigrants should respect the host country's laws and heritage. *Quiz area:* i1–i3. The catechism holds both sides of the quiz's immigration line in one paragraph.

## 4. Ancient Rome itself: the mixed constitution

- **Polybius**, a Greek historian of Rome, described Rome's government as "mixed." Consuls (monarchical), Senate (aristocratic), and popular assemblies (democratic) each checked the others.
- John Adams leaned on Polybius in his *Defence of the Constitutions*, which circulated at the 1787 Convention. Madison drew on him for his notes on ancient confederacies (Natelson, 2022).
- That line runs into **Federalist No. 51** (Madison, 1788): "Ambition must be made to counteract ambition."
- **Cicero** (*De Re Publica*, *De Legibus*) gave the West a lasting idea of natural law, a justice above any one ruler's will. Later thinkers drew on it for limits on government.
- **Quiz area:** g2 emergency powers (checks and balances versus fast action), g1 and g3 (national versus spread-out power).
- Caution: people across the spectrum invoke checks and balances, usually against whichever side holds power. That's one reason government power is left out of the rough left–right summary.

## 5. Foundational modern philosophers by quiz area

| Thinker | Core idea (short) | Areas | Pulls toward |
|---|---|---|---|
| John Locke (1689) | Government rests on consent and protects life, liberty, and property | Economic, government power | Limited government; also grounds the right to resist |
| Edmund Burke (1790) | Change should be gradual; traditions carry inherited wisdom | Social, government power | Caution about rapid change |
| J. S. Mill, *On Liberty* (1859) | The state may limit liberty only to prevent harm to others | Civil liberties, social, speech | Personal liberty; the harm side can justify rules too |
| John Rawls (1971) | Choose rules as if you didn't know your place in society | Economic, public services | Help for the worst off |
| Robert Nozick (1974) | Fair holdings come from fair acquisition and transfer | Economic, taxes | Limits on redistribution |

Notice that Mill is claimed by civil libertarians on the left and the right. Locke is cited both for property rights and for the right to overthrow unjust government. These thinkers don't line up neatly either.

## 6. Rules this guide adds to the review

1. **Steelman both sides.** Each statement's reason should be one its supporters would sign. Check against section 2's audit after any edit.
2. **No authorities in questions.** No thinkers, scripture, parties, or popes named in any statement.
3. **No group predictions.** Never say or imply that Christians, Catholics, or followers of any thinker answer a certain way. The sources show the opposite.
4. **"Where these ideas come from" panel (built Oct 25, 2026).** It sits after the results, collapsed, and lists sources toward each end of every area plus sources both sides claim. It is never tied to a visitor's answers. Gun policy, trade, and foreign policy were added with Second Amendment and *Heller*, Smith and Hamilton, and Washington, NATO Article 5, and the just war tradition.
5. **Scripture accuracy.** If scripture is ever shown, quote a named translation and give chapter and verse. Don't paraphrase it in ways that tilt the meaning.
